\large A formal model of Unison's content addressing and update scheme}

\begin{abstract}
Unison is a programming language whose codebase is content-addressed: a definition is identified by
the hash of its syntax, names are metadata attached to hashes rather than part of the code, and a
computed result is cached against a hash and never invalidated. These properties are widely cited.
As far as we can determine they have never been formally stated, and Unison has no peer-reviewed
publication, so the implementation is the only citable description of what it guarantees.

This document states them. We give a small object language --- the simply-typed $\lambda$-calculus
with $\TNat$ and $\TText$ --- and model, faithfully to the implementation at a pinned commit: hashing a strongly connected component of mutually recursive definitions; references as
$(\mathit{hash}, \mathit{position})$ pairs; reference transparency; the type inside the term hash;
the namespace as a relation in which conflicts are representable; the watch cache; and
\texttt{update}, which propagates an edit by rendering dependents to source, re-parsing, and
re-typechecking them; and \texttt{merge}, which reconciles two independently edited namespaces by
comparing a second hash computed from names rather than from content.

Three results are perhaps not obvious from the source. \textbf{(i)} Presentation-independence of
component hashing is \emph{conditional}: when two members of a recursive cycle are structurally
equivalent modulo intra-cycle references, the component's hash depends on the members' \emph{names}.
The condition is decidable and Unison computes it, aborting rather than storing such a hash --- at the
top level. One nesting level down, inside a \texttt{let rec}, the same condition is computed and
discarded, and the enclosing definition's identity silently depends on the order its bindings were
written in.
\textbf{(ii)} Reference transparency holds exactly at position $0$ of a component; other members are
referenced opaquely, so transparency and the component model are not independent features.
\textbf{(iii)} \texttt{update}'s correctness rests on printing-then-parsing preserving hashes, a
property the implementation does not state and maintains with a golden-file regression corpus.
\textbf{(iv)} \texttt{merge} decides whether a definition was edited by a human by asking whether it
\emph{renders} differently, so the guarantee that an untouched definition cannot conflict holds only
while names are stable. Renaming defeats it in three distinct ways: a conflict where there is no
disagreement, a failure with no conflict reported, and a clean merge that silently undoes the rename.

A closing section compares the design with \emph{arbor}, a content-addressed substrate the authors
are developing, for which a companion formalization exists. That section is the only place arbor
appears; the rest of the document is about Unison alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:uscope}

\subsection{What Unison does differently}

In a conventional language a definition is identified by its name and located in a file. In Unison a
definition is identified by the hash of its own syntax, with names --- of its binders and of the
other definitions it mentions --- playing no part in that identity. The consequences that follow are
the system's design:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Names are metadata.} Renaming a definition does not change it, because its identity
        never mentioned the name. Nothing downstream is invalidated by a rename.
  \item \textbf{Definitions are immutable.} ``Editing'' a definition produces a different hash, so it
        is a different definition; existing callers continue to refer to the old one until something
        deliberately points them elsewhere.
  \item \textbf{Caches need no invalidation.} A computed value is stored against the hash of the
        expression that produced it. Since that hash denotes one fixed expression forever, the entry
        cannot go stale.
\end{itemize}

Mutually recursive definitions are the exception that shapes everything in \cref{sec:ustore}. A
member of a cycle cannot carry its neighbours' hashes, because computing any of them requires all of
them; so a cycle is hashed as a \emph{unit}, with its internal references standing as indices rather
than hashes. That single exception is why the store maps hashes to components rather than to
definitions, why a reference is a pair, and why \cref{def:comphash} needs a canonical ordering at
all.

The mechanism that makes this work also creates the rest of the problems the system has to solve, and
those are what this document is mostly about: names have to be reattached to hashes when code is
displayed, and propagating an edit to the code that depends on it is not a rename but a rebuild.

\subsection{Why formalize it}

Unison's claims about identity, immutability and caching are stated in its documentation and blog
posts as design intent. What is not available anywhere is a statement of them precise enough to
check, or to compare against another design --- what exactly is being identified with what, under
which conditions, and what has to hold for the conclusions to follow. Absent a publication, the
implementation is the specification, and the implementation is roughly 161{,}000 lines of Haskell.

We wrote this in order to compare Unison with a system we are building (\cref{sec:contrast}), and it
proved worth writing on its own terms. Several of the observations below --- the position-$0$
boundary of \cref{prop:transparent}, the two orderings of \cref{rem:twoorders}, the three
representations of \cref{rem:unhash} --- concern behaviour the code fixes precisely but which no
prose description we have found states. Writing down what a system does, in a form precise enough to
prove things about, turns out to be a different activity from reading it, and to surface different
things.

\subsection{Summary of results}\label{sec:summary}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \Cref{prop:transparent} --- reference transparency holds exactly at position $0$.
  \item \Cref{def:comphash}, \cref{rem:twoorders} --- component hashing computes two distinct
        orderings of the same component, one fixing de Bruijn indices and one fixing stored
        positions.
  \item \Cref{thm:ordering} --- presentation-independence of component hashing is conditional. When
        the condition fails, which member is referenced transparently always depends on the members'
        names, and the component's hash usually does.
  \item \Cref{cor:detect}, \cref{rem:notpartial} --- the condition is decidable, is computed, and at
        the top level the implementation aborts rather than store such a hash.
  \item \Cref{rem:innercycle} --- inside a \texttt{let rec} the same condition is computed and
        discarded, and the enclosing definition's identity then depends on the order the bindings were
        written in. The guarantee above is a guarantee about top-level components, not about hashing.
  \item \Cref{thm:ualpha} --- hashing identifies terms up to $\alpha$-equivalence and cycle
        permutation, and no further.
  \item \Cref{rem:unhash} --- a cyclic definition is stored in a different form from the one that
        was hashed, with self-references carrying no hash; an explicit inverse bridges them.
  \item \Cref{cor:wnoinval} --- no cache invalidation is required, as a consequence of immutability
        rather than as a heuristic.
  \item \Cref{def:usave}, \cref{rem:saveident} --- identity is conferred by \emph{typechecking},
        on every save, and storing and naming are a separate later gesture. So the evaluation cache
        is populated with no commit involved.
  \item \Cref{prop:watchdom} --- a save mints identities for things nothing ever stores, so the
        cache can be keyed by a hash that denotes nothing in the store. The cache's soundness
        argument never depended on the key being a definition.
  \item \Cref{prop:print-parse} --- \texttt{update} is correct only if printing inverts parsing.
  \item \Cref{prop:follow-only} --- \texttt{update} admits exactly one propagation strategy.
  \item \Cref{prop:merge-store} --- two independently grown stores union without reconciliation, which
        is the formal content of ``content-addressed codebases have no merge conflicts''. Nothing a
        user calls a conflict lives there.
  \item \Cref{def:synhash}, \cref{rem:syn-dual} --- merge's syntactic hash is the exact dual of the
        content hash: same recursion, names substituted for references instead of the reverse. It is
        never stored.
  \item \Cref{rem:syn-only} --- once two syntactic hashes differ, no content hash is consulted again.
        Merges are decided by name.
  \item \Cref{thm:noconflict}, \cref{cor:noedit} --- a definition nobody authored cannot conflict,
        conditionally: every dependency it gained must keep the name the merge gives it.
  \item \Cref{cor:noedit-user} --- the same statement in terms of what people did. If neither party
        moves, aliases or deletes a name, then nobody is ever asked about a definition nobody wrote.
        Contrapositively (\cref{rem:onlyrenames}): renaming is the only user action that can do it.
  \item \Cref{def:gestures}, \cref{rem:renamefree} --- renaming costs one row and cannot break a
        caller or a cached result, which is the usual argument for content addressing. Merge is the
        one operation that reads names as though they were content, and is where that stops.
  \item \Cref{prop:rename-conflict}, \cref{cor:unchecked} --- renaming defeats that condition, and
        unlike \cref{cor:detect} the condition is neither checked nor reported. The information that
        would discharge it is computed and passed to a debug printer.
  \item \Cref{prop:merge-asym} --- merge is not commutative: the same two branches can merge cleanly
        in one direction and fail in the other, with no conflict either way.
  \item \Cref{prop:merge-print-parse} --- merge's round trip renders under one namespace and parses
        under another, and nothing requires the names it emits to exist in the second.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What this document is, and is not}

It is a model of an existing system, faithful to the implementation at commit \texttt{db60ce2}. It
proposes no changes. Where we describe something as a cost or a limitation we mean it descriptively:
every design pays somewhere, and pointing at where is not the same as saying it is wrong. The one
condition we identify that Unison treats as an error (\cref{cor:detect}) is already known and
tracked upstream as issue \#2787, and the implementation's handling of it --- detect and abort ---
is in our view the right response.

We are not modeling Unison's language. There are no abilities, no data declarations, no pattern
matching, no ANF runtime. \Cref{sec:unotmodeled} lists what is left out. Anything a Unison developer
finds surprising here is more likely to be our error than a discovery, and we would welcome
correction.

\subsection{The object language, and why it is typed}

The object language is the simply-typed $\lambda$-calculus with $\TNat$, $\TText$ and references
--- deliberately small, and typed for one reason. Unison's
\texttt{update} re-typechecks the code it rebuilds, and the interesting half of \texttt{update} is
what happens when that \emph{fails} --- Unison removes the broken definitions from the namespace and
hands them back to the user as editable source. Without a type system that branch is unreachable and
the most distinctive thing about the operation cannot be stated.

\subsection{What is modeled}

Stated as the operations a user can perform and what each one is observed to do, rather than as a
list of internal structures.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Ingest.} You present a group of definitions written in the object language. The
        system gives each one an identity derived from its content and stores it. Definitions that
        refer to one another cyclically receive their identities \emph{together}, which is the source
        of most of what follows.
  \item \textbf{Reference.} A stored definition may mention others. What it mentions is fixed when it
        is stored and cannot afterwards change, so no edit anywhere alters what an existing
        definition means.
  \item \textbf{Naming.} Names are attached to identities from outside, and are not part of what is
        stored. A name may denote more than one thing at once, and the system continues to function
        when it does.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation and caching.} A result is remembered against the identity of the
        expression that produced it, and never has to be discarded.
  \item \textbf{Update.} You present a new version of a definition. The system works out what
        depended on the old one, rebuilds those, and either accepts the result or tells you what
        broke --- by handing the broken definitions back to you as source.
  \item \textbf{Merge.} Two people edit from a common starting point and the results are combined.
        The system decides, definition by definition, whether each side's version was written by a
        person or produced by rebuilding, and asks the user only about the ones both people wrote.
\end{itemize}

Two things visible only through their consequences are modeled as well, because the rest does not
make sense without them: how the identity of a mutually recursive group is fixed
(\cref{def:comphash}), and the fact that a definition's recorded type is part of its identity
(\cref{def:typeinhash}).

\subsection{Not modeled}\label{sec:unotmodeled}

Stated so the boundary is explicit rather than implied.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{The namespace's representation.} Unison's \texttt{Branch} is a hashed Merkle tree of
        name segments whose \texttt{children} field points at each child's \emph{causal} hash, so
        every level of the tree has its own history \src{branchh:22-39}. Here the namespace
        is a bare relation. \Cref{sec:unaming} models the naming \emph{semantics}, which
        \texttt{update} needs; it does not model the object that carries them.
  \item \textbf{History and branching.} \texttt{Causal} as a content-addressed DAG, and with it the
        computation of the common ancestor of two branches. \Cref{sec:umerge} models merge as a
        function of three namespaces and takes the ancestor as given.
  \item \textbf{Suffix resolution and the \texttt{lib}-depth priority rule.} Names here are atomic;
        in Unison a name is a sequence of segments and may be abbreviated to any unambiguous suffix.
  \item \textbf{Data declarations and \texttt{unique}-type minting.} With only $\TNat$, $\TText$ and
        arrows there are no declarations, so the $\UCon{\cdot}{\cdot}$ arm of $\Referent$ is present
        but vestigial, and the minting axis is absent entirely.
  \item \textbf{Abilities, the ANF runtime, \texttt{LocalIds}, and the serialization layer.}
\end{itemize}

\section{The object language}\label{sec:ulang}

There are two grammars, and keeping them apart is the point of this section. A term as \emph{written}
mentions other definitions by name, and names are variables. A term as \emph{stored} mentions them by
reference, and a reference carries a hash. Name resolution is the map between the two, and it is
partial in a way that matters later.

In the implementation these are one datatype with one set of constructors; the distinction is which
constructors occur at which stage. Splitting them here is an idealization, and it is the idealization
that makes the rest of the document legible.

\subsection{Source terms}

\begin{definition}[Source terms]\label{def:uterm}
Fix a countable set of variables $x,y,z$.
\[
  e \;::=\; \uvar{x} \;\mid\; \ulam{x}{e} \;\mid\; \uapp{e}{e'}
        \;\mid\; \unat{n} \;\mid\; \utext{s} \;\mid\; \uann{e}{T}
\]
This is what a user writes. Note what is absent: there is no way to write a hash, and no term former
denotes a stored definition. A mention of another definition is a \emph{free variable}, exactly as a
mention of a local binding is a bound one, and the two are told apart only by an environment.
\end{definition}

\subsection{References}

\begin{definition}[Reference]\label{def:uref}
\src{ref:77-119} A reference denotes something already stored.
\[
  r \;::=\; \UBuiltin{b} \;\mid\; \UDer{h}{i}
  \qquad b \in \TText,\; h \in \Hash,\; i \in \Pos = \mathbb{N}
\]
$\UBuiltin{b}$ names a primitive, by text rather than by hash. $\UDer{h}{i}$ names the $i$-th member
of the group stored at $h$; \cref{def:ustore} explains why that is a pair.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Primitives are named extrinsically]\label{rem:builtin}
An identifier is \emph{intrinsic} when it can be recomputed from the thing it names and
\emph{extrinsic} when it is an assertion by some authority; a hash is the former and a builtin name
is the latter. So the system is intrinsically identified everywhere except at its primitives, and the
boundary is visible in the type of a reference rather than hidden in a convention. This is not
avoidable --- a hash chain must bottom out at things whose content the system does not model --- and
it is worth noting only because the sum type makes the seam unusually legible.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Resolved terms}

\begin{definition}[Resolved terms]\label{def:urterm}
\[
  t \;::=\; \uvar{x} \;\mid\; \ulam{x}{t} \;\mid\; \uapp{t}{u}
        \;\mid\; \unat{n} \;\mid\; \utext{s} \;\mid\; \uref{r} \;\mid\; \uann{t}{T}
\]
\Cref{def:uterm} extended with references. Binders still carry \emph{names}, and those names are
stored --- \cref{def:uhash} erases them only while hashing.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$\alpha$-equivalence]\label{def:ualpha}
$t \equiv_\alpha u$ is the least congruence identifying $\ulam{x}{t}$ with $\ulam{y}{t[x := y]}$ for
$y$ fresh in $t$. In the implementation it is the equality on terms, so $\alpha$-equivalent terms are
\emph{equal} before any hashing takes place.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Why storing binder names matters]\label{rem:names}
Storing de Bruijn indices and inventing names for display would produce the same hashes, so the
choice looks free. It is not. \Cref{sec:uupdate}'s \texttt{update} renders stored definitions back to
source that a human reads and a parser resolves, and because names survive storage, what is rendered
uses the \emph{author's} variable names. A system that discarded them could state its round trip only
up to a choice of names.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Name resolution]\label{def:uresolution}
\src{fparse:185-191; coreterm:172-176} Given a namespace $\UNames$ (\cref{def:unames}) and a set $B$ of
names bound by the enclosing binding group, resolution $\mathcal{R}^{B}_{\UNames}$ maps a source term
to a resolved term, homomorphically except at variables:
\[
  \mathcal{R}^{B}_{\UNames}(\uvar{x}) \;=\;
    \begin{cases}
      \uvar{x} & x \in B \text{, or } x \text{ locally bound}\\
      \uref{r} & \UNames(x) = \{\URefT{r}\}\\
      \uvar{x} & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Resolution is deliberately partial]\label{rem:staging}
Three of these cases leave a variable in place, and only one produces a reference. That is not an
omission.

A name bound by the group being ingested \emph{cannot} be resolved --- its referent does not exist
yet, and for a recursive group it cannot exist until the group is hashed (\cref{sec:comphash}).
Resolution therefore stops at the boundary of what is already stored, and the remaining variables are
discharged by ingest.

The last case is more interesting: a name that resolves to nothing, or to more than one thing, is
left free \emph{on purpose}. The implementation's comments are explicit \src{coreterm:175-176} --- ``not found: leave free
for telling user about expected type'' and ``ambiguous: leave free for TDNR'' --- so an unresolved
name survives into typechecking, where the expected type may disambiguate it or produce a better
error than resolution could. Failure to resolve is not an error in itself.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Types and typing}

\begin{definition}[Types and typing]\label{def:utyping}
\[
  T,U \;::=\; \TNat \;\mid\; \TText \;\mid\; \uarr{T}{U}
\]
Typing is over \emph{resolved} terms. A context $\Gamma$ maps variables to types; a signature
$\Delta$ maps references to types --- given for builtins, and read off the store for derived
references (\cref{def:typeinhash}). Syntax-directed, with no inference:
\begin{mathpar}
  \inferrule*[right=U-Var]{\Gamma(x) = T}{\utypes{\Gamma}{\uvar{x}}{T}}
  \and
  \inferrule*[right=U-Nat]{\ }{\utypes{\Gamma}{\unat{n}}{\TNat}}
  \and
  \inferrule*[right=U-Text]{\ }{\utypes{\Gamma}{\utext{s}}{\TText}}
  \\
  \inferrule*[right=U-Abs]{\utypes{\Gamma, x{:}T}{t}{U}}{\utypes{\Gamma}{\ulam{x}{t}}{\uarr{T}{U}}}
  \and
  \inferrule*[right=U-App]{\utypes{\Gamma}{t}{\uarr{T}{U}} \\ \utypes{\Gamma}{u}{T}}
                          {\utypes{\Gamma}{\uapp{t}{u}}{U}}
  \\
  \inferrule*[right=U-Ref]{\Delta(r) = T}{\utypes{\Gamma}{\uref{r}}{T}}
  \and
  \inferrule*[right=U-Ann]{\utypes{\Gamma}{t}{T}}{\utypes{\Gamma}{\uann{t}{T}}{T}}
\end{mathpar}
A term with a free variable is not typeable, which is how \cref{rem:staging}'s unresolved names
become errors --- at typechecking, with a type to report, rather than at resolution.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Binding groups}

\begin{definition}[Binding group]\label{def:bindgroup}
A \emph{binding group} is a finite map $\bg$ from names to resolved terms, whose members' free
variables all lie in $\dom(\bg)$. Ingest takes a binding group, not a single term.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[File]\label{def:ufile}
\src{ufile:270-290} A \emph{file} is a pair $F = (\bg, \overline{w})$ of a binding group and a
finite sequence of \emph{watch expressions} $\overline{w} = w_1,\dots,w_m$ --- resolved terms with
no name, whose free variables also lie in $\dom(\bg)$. What the user edits is a file; what
\cref{def:bindgroup} describes is only half of it.

The distinction is not a matter of presentation. A watch is anonymous, so nothing can ever refer to
it, so it is always its own singleton component; but it is closed over the group's names exactly as a
definition is, and \cref{def:uingest} hashes it in the same pass. The implementation is explicit
that the two travel together --- \texttt{allTerms = join tlcs ++ join (snd <\$> watches)}, under a
comment reading ``includes watches''.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Why ingest is group-at-a-time]\label{rem:mutrec}
There is no recursive term former. Mutual recursion is several bindings that mention one another, so
the unit that can be given identities is the group, not the definition. Everything distinctive about
\cref{sec:ustore} follows from this: with single-term ingest every component would be a singleton and
\cref{def:comphash} would be invisible.

The closedness condition is what \cref{rem:staging} left over --- the variables resolution could not
discharge are exactly the group's own names, and ingest discharges them by hashing.
\end{remark}

\section{The store and the hash}\label{sec:ustore}

\subsection{The store and its components}

\begin{definition}[Component and store]\label{def:ustore}
 A \emph{component} is a
non-empty finite sequence of terms, $\Comp = \Term^{+}$ --- a strongly connected component of the
dependency graph, hashed as a unit. The store is
\[
  \UStore : \Hash \pfn \Comp .
\]
A definition is addressed by a \emph{pair}: $\UDer{h}{i}$ denotes the $i$-th member of the component
at $h$. A non-recursive definition is a singleton component with $i = 0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[The store is not hash-to-definition]\label{rem:storetype}
It is natural to summarize content addressing as ``a definition is identified by its hash'', and for
non-recursive code that is what happens. But the map is from a hash to a \emph{component}, and a
definition is a pair. Mutual recursion is the reason: a member of a cycle cannot be hashed before its
neighbours, since it refers to them, and they refer back. Hashing the cycle as a unit breaks the
knot, at the price of making every reference positional and every property below a property of
components rather than of definitions.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Hashing}

\begin{definition}[Token stream]\label{def:tokens}
\src{tokenizable:122-137} Nothing is hashed as raw bytes. The input is a tagged,
length-prefixed token stream: $\mathsf{Tag}$ is one byte; $\mathsf{Text}$ and $\mathsf{Bytes}$ are a
64-bit big-endian length followed by the payload; $\mathsf{Nat}$ is 64-bit big-endian;
$\mathsf{Hashed}$ is a fixed-width hash with no length prefix. Every stream begins with a
\emph{version} token, and each syntactic layer begins with a tag domain-separating it from the others
\src{term:147-149; type:136-139}. Write $\mathsf{acc}(\overline{\tau})$ for the hash of a token list.
The function is SHA3-512, untruncated.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Versioning the hash function]\label{rem:version}
The leading version token exists so that changing the hash function changes \emph{every} hash --- if
it did not, simple values would collide across generations in a single global hash table. Underneath,
the schema carries a many-hashes-to-one-object indirection so that hashes computed under an old
scheme keep resolving --- ``if the hashing algorithm for the object is changed'' \src{sql:33-49}.
This is a comparatively rare thing for a content-addressed system to plan for, and worth recording:
the intended migration story for ``we changed the hash'' is versioning plus indirection, not
recomputation. It is provisioned rather than exercised: every write hardcodes version $2$, with a
standing note to revisit ``once there are v1 and v2 hashes'' \src{queries:778}.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Hashing a term]\label{def:uhash}
\src{abt:145-174} Hashing carries an environment
$\HEnv \in (\wp(\Name) + \Name)^{*}$, innermost first, where $\mathsf{R}(x)$ is an ordinary binder and
$\mathsf{L}(V)$ is a \emph{cycle frame} naming a whole set at once. Write $\uhash{\HEnv}{t}$.
\begin{align*}
  \uhash{\HEnv}{\uvar{x}}    &= \mathsf{acc}[\mathsf{Nat}\; j]
     && \text{$j$ least with $x \in \HEnv_j$; undefined if none}\\
  \uhash{\HEnv}{\ulam{x}{t}} &= \mathsf{acc}[\ldots,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\;\uhash{\mathsf{R}(x)\cdot\HEnv}{t}]\\
  \uhash{\HEnv}{\uapp{t}{u}} &= \mathsf{acc}[\ldots,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\;\uhash{\HEnv}{t},\ \mathsf{Hashed}\;\uhash{\HEnv}{u}]
\end{align*}
and correspondingly at the other formers. Three details are load-bearing. Binder \emph{names are
discarded} on the way in, so $\alpha$-equivalent terms hash equally. \emph{Source annotations are excluded by
construction} --- ``we ignore annotations in the \texttt{Term}, as these should never affect the
meaning of the term'' \src{abt:136-137}. The word is the implementation's, and it means the ABT's
annotation parameter, which carries source spans; a \emph{type} ascription $\uann{t}{T}$ is a term
former and \emph{is} hashed, which is what makes \cref{def:typeinhash} work at all. And a variable
bound by a cycle frame
$\mathsf{L}(V)$ hashes to the frame's index, \emph{the same index for every member of $V$}; that is
what \cref{def:comphash} exploits. A free variable is an error.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The type is inside the term hash]\label{def:typeinhash}
\src{term:92-109} Before hashing, each member of a binding group is wrapped in its type,
$t \mapsto \uann{t}{T}$, replacing an existing top-level annotation if there is one. The hash of a
definition is therefore a function of its type as well as its body, and a change of signature is a
change of identity.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[The exception that proves the rule]\label{rem:typeinhash}
There is a \texttt{hashTermComponentsWithoutTypes} \src{term:120-122} used where no type is
available, and its own caller is warned off: ``this shouldn't be used when storing terms in the
codebase, as it doesn't incorporate the type into the hash'' \src{convert:87-89}. Its existence is
the clearest statement that the usual path deliberately puts the type in: hashing without the type is
possible, and is treated as the special case.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[\textsc{U-Ref} is a lookup, not a re-derivation]\label{rem:uref-delta}
Typing a reference costs nothing: $\Delta$ is consulted and no derivation about the target is
required. That is affordable precisely because the type is inside the hash
(\cref{def:typeinhash}) --- a stored definition's recorded type cannot disagree with the definition,
since changing the type would change the hash and therefore produce a different definition. The store
admits no ill-typed entry, so there is nothing for \textsc{U-Ref} to check.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Reference transparency, and its boundary}

\begin{proposition}[Transparency holds exactly at position $0$]\label{prop:transparent}
\src{term:135-146} In the term layer,
\[
  \uhash{\HEnv}{\uref{\UDer{h}{0}}} = h,
  \qquad
  \uhash{\HEnv}{\uref{\UDer{h}{i}}} = \mathsf{acc}[\mathsf{Tag}\;1,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\;h,\ \mathsf{Nat}\;i]
  \quad (i \neq 0).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[What transparency does, and where its boundary falls]\label{rem:transparent}
For $i = 0$ --- every non-recursive definition, and one distinguished member of each cycle
(\cref{sec:comphash} assigns the positions) --- a reference contributes \emph{literally} the
referent's hash. A reference to position $0$ is therefore interchangeable, inside any enclosing hash,
with any term that hashes to $h$.

For $i \neq 0$ it is opaque, tagged with its position. So transparency and the component model are
not independent features; the boundary between them falls at $\Pos = 0$. Whether that is deliberate
--- inlining a singleton component is free, so there is a plausible reason --- or an artifact of the
encoding, we do not know; the source comment justifies only the $0$ case.

\Cref{ex:transparency} works through what this does and does not give, and the answer is narrower
than the usual summary of it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Component hashing}\label{sec:comphash}

This is the deepest mechanism in the document and the one whose properties are least apparent from
the code.

\begin{definition}[Component hashing]\label{def:comphash}
\src{abt:73-100, 175-217} Let $C = \langle (y_1,u_1),\dots,(y_k,u_k)\rangle$ be a
component, \emph{listed in name order} --- the implementation always obtains it as
$\mathsf{Map.toList}$.\footnote{Strictly the order is on $(\mathit{freshening\ id}, \mathit{var\
kind})$ rather than on the name; it coincides with alphabetical order here because top-level bindings
have their freshening id reset to $0$ and all carry the same var kind. The distinction matters on the
re-hash path of \cref{rem:unhash}, where the variables are machine-generated from stored positions
and the listing is therefore by $\Pos$: re-hashing agrees with hashing precisely because
\cref{thm:ordering}(a)'s side condition makes the two listings induce the same $\pi$.} Let
$V = \{y_1,\dots,y_k\}$.

\emph{Step 1 --- permutation hashes.} In the environment $\HEnv_p = \mathsf{L}(V)$, every intra-cycle
reference resolves to the same index, so member bodies become comparable without any of them yet
having an identity:
\[
  \phash{y_j} \;=\; \uhash{\HEnv_p}{u_j}.
\]

\emph{Step 2 --- the binding order.} Sort the members by $\phash{\cdot}$, \textbf{stably}, giving a
permutation $\pi$; set $\HEnv = \mathsf{R}(y_{\pi(1)})\cdots\mathsf{R}(y_{\pi(k)})$. Each member now
has a distinct de Bruijn index, and the member hashes are $h_j = \uhash{\HEnv}{u_{\pi(j)}}$.

\emph{Step 3 --- the storage order.} With
$\overline{\tau} = \mathsf{Tag}\;1,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\;h_1,\dots,\mathsf{Hashed}\;h_k$ shared by every
member, put
\[
  \nhash{y} \;=\; \mathsf{acc}\bigl(\overline{\tau} \;+\!\!+\; [\mathsf{Hashed}\;\uhash{\HEnv}{\uvar{y}}]\bigr),
\]
so a member is disambiguated \emph{only} by its own index in $\HEnv$. Sort the members by
$\nhash{\cdot}$; that order is the component's storage order, the position $i$ in $\UDer{h}{i}$
counts in it, and
\[
  \comphash(C) \;=\; \mathsf{acc}[\mathsf{Hashed}\;\nhash{y_{\sigma(1)}},\dots,\mathsf{Hashed}\;\nhash{y_{\sigma(k)}}]
\]
for $\sigma$ the sorting permutation.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Two orders, not one]\label{rem:twoorders}
$\pi$ and $\sigma$ are different permutations of the same component. $\pi$ fixes the de Bruijn
indices used \emph{while hashing} member bodies; $\sigma$ fixes the $\Pos$ indices that appear \emph{in
stored references}. They coincide only by accident, since $\sigma$ sorts by a hash of the $\pi$-index.
Nothing observable depends on the difference --- re-hashing re-derives $\HEnv$ from scratch --- but a
reimplementation that conflated them would produce different hashes for the same code, and no prose
description of the algorithm we are aware of mentions that there are two.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Ingest]\label{def:uingest}
\src{abt:106-134; ufile:274-284} $\hashcomps(F)$ decomposes $\bg \cup \overline{w}$ into strongly
connected components in topological order and folds \cref{def:comphash} over them, substituting
$\UDer{h}{i}$ for the names of already-hashed components before hashing the next. It returns a
reference for \emph{every} member, definitions and watches alike.

$\hashcomps$ \emph{only} computes references. It extends neither $\UStore$ nor $\UNames$; those are
the business of the two transitions below, and separating them is not tidiness --- see
\cref{def:usave}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Save]\label{def:usave}
\src{load:464-494; ufile:270-290} \emph{Saving} a file is the transition
\[
  \UConfig{\UStore}{\UNames}{\UCache}
  \;\longrightarrow\;
  \UConfig{\UStore}{\UNames}{\UCache'}
\]
given by: typecheck $F$; if it fails, stop. Otherwise compute $\hashcomps(F)$, and for each watch
$w_j$ with reference $r_j$, set $\UCache' = \UCache[r_j \mapsto v_j]$ where $v_j$ is $w_j$'s value
--- evaluated against $\UStore$ \emph{together with} the definitions $\hashcomps$ just gave
references to, and \textbf{only when $r_j \notin \dom(\UCache)$ already}.

Note the two components that do not move. $\UStore$ is unchanged: a save stores nothing.
$\UNames$ is unchanged: a save names nothing. What a save does is \emph{confer identity} and
\emph{populate the cache}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Identity is conferred by typechecking, not by committing]\label{rem:saveident}
It is tempting --- and this document did it until the distinction was pointed out --- to read the
commit gate of \cref{rem:mutrec} as saying that a definition acquires its identity when it is
\emph{added}. It does not. It acquires its identity when the file \emph{typechecks}, which happens
on every save, and the implementation keeps it: a \texttt{TypecheckedUnisonFile} carries a
reference $r$ (\cref{def:uref}) for every member in its \texttt{hashTermsId} field, and nothing has
been added to anything. \Cref{def:update} then \emph{stores and binds} what already has a hash.

Two consequences that are hard to see otherwise. First, the gate is a gate on \emph{identity}, not on
storage --- which is why a buffer that does not typecheck has nothing to talk about at all, rather
than merely nothing stored. Second, the caching of \cref{sec:ucache} happens at save time, so
re-saving an unchanged expensive watch is free \emph{with no commit anywhere in the picture}. That is
observable: on \texttt{ucm} release/1.1.1 one expensive watch costs 5.79\,s, the same watch saved
twice costs 5.82\,s, and two distinct watches of equal cost 11.45\,s.

The implementation makes the hit/miss distinction explicit rather than implicit, which is the clearest
evidence that this is the intended reading: \texttt{evaluateWatches} returns an
$\mathit{isHit}$ flag per watch, and the save path writes the cache
``only when there are no errors'' and $\mathsf{not}\ \mathit{isHit}$ \src{load:490-493}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Where a watch parts company from a definition]\label{rem:watchsplit}
Both are hashed by the same call, so up to \cref{def:usave} they are treated alike. They diverge only
at \cref{def:update}: a definition's component is added to $\UStore$ and its name bound in $\UNames$;
a watch's is added to neither, and only its value survives, in $\UCache$ at the reference the save
computed. So the system mints identities for things it never stores, which is \cref{prop:watchdom}.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[The cache's domain is not contained in the store's]\label{prop:watchdom}
There are configurations reachable by \cref{def:update} in which
$\dom(\UCache) \not\subseteq \{\,\UDer{h}{i} \mid h \in \dom(\UStore)\,\}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Save} (\cref{def:usave}) a file whose binding group is empty and whose watch list is a single
closed expression $w$. Then $w$ receives a reference $\UDer{h}{0}$ and $\UCache$ is extended at it,
while $\UStore$ is unchanged by definition. Note that no commit is required, or even possible to
help: \cref{def:update} adds components for the file's \emph{definitions}, of which there are none.
Since $w$ is unnamed, no later operation can reach it either --- $\tdeps$ ranges over named
references (\cref{def:uscope}), and $\render$ has no name to print it by (\cref{def:ppe}). So
$h \notin \dom(\UStore)$ permanently.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[A cyclic definition is not stored in the form that was hashed]\label{rem:unhash}
Three representations of an intra-component reference have to be kept apart, and the difference is
invisible unless one looks for it.

\emph{While hashing}, a reference from one cycle member to another is a \textbf{variable}, resolved
by \cref{def:uhash} to an index into the cycle frame. It has to be: the component's hash is what is
being computed.

\emph{In memory}, once \cref{def:uingest} has finished, those variables have been substituted by
$\UDer{h}{i}$ carrying the component's own freshly computed hash.

\emph{Serialized}, a self-reference is stored with \textbf{no hash at all}: the hash field becomes
optional, and the absent case marks a reference into the enclosing component. The type is named for
it --- ``a possibly-self (R = \emph{recursive}) reference'' \src{ref:51-52} --- and the storage
format says what the absence means: ``an added distinguished element (here \texttt{Nothing}) which
represents a self-reference'' \src{tformat:19-23}.

Consequently the bytes on disk are \emph{not} the bytes that were hashed, and re-deriving a stored
component's hash requires undoing the substitution first. That operation exists and is documented as
such: \texttt{unhashComponent} \src{cterm:265-276} --- whose declaration analogue is documented as
``replaces all \texttt{Nothing} self-referential hashes with a variable reference to the relevant
piece of the component'' \src{decl:81-83} --- and the hash-validation path calls it before re-hashing
and compares \src{termhash:26-45}.

None of this is observable through the API, and it is a sensible encoding --- writing a component's
own hash into its members' serialized bytes would mean the bytes could not be produced until the hash
was known, which is the circularity the whole construction exists to break. It is worth stating
because ``the hash of a definition is the hash of what is stored'' is a natural thing to assume, and
for cyclic definitions it is false in three distinct ways.
\end{remark}

\subsection{What component hashing guarantees}

\begin{theorem}[Conditional presentation-independence]\label{thm:ordering}
Let $C$ and $C'$ be the same component listed in two different orders.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\alph*)]
  \item If $\phash{\cdot}$ is \emph{injective} on $V$, then $\comphash(C) = \comphash(C')$, the member
        hashes agree, and each member receives the same $\Pos$.
  \item If $\phash{\cdot}$ is not injective, the tied members' de Bruijn indices are decided by the
        listed order, and hence --- since the list is always obtained in name order --- by the
        members' \textbf{names}. Two consequences, of different strengths:
        \begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(b\arabic*)]
          \item \emph{Always}: which member receives which $\Pos$ depends on the names. Renaming a
                member can change what $\UDer{h}{0}$ denotes, and therefore which member of the cycle
                is referenced transparently (\cref{prop:transparent}).
          \item \emph{In general, but not for every tie}: $\comphash(C)$ itself depends on the names.
        \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$\HEnv_p$ mentions no member individually, so $\phash{y}$ is a function of $u_y$ alone and is
unaffected by the listing. (a) If $\phash{\cdot}$ is injective, sorting by it is a total order on $V$
determined by the members' bodies, so $\pi$, hence $\HEnv$, hence every $h_j$, hence every
$\nhash{\cdot}$, hence $\sigma$ and $\comphash$, are all listing-independent. (b) If
$\phash{y} = \phash{y'}$ for $y \neq y'$, the sort in Step 2 is stable and therefore returns them in
listed order; exchanging them in the listing exchanges their de Bruijn indices. That gives (b1)
immediately, since $\HEnv$ is what $\nhash{\cdot}$, hence $\sigma$, hence $\Pos$, is computed from.
It does not give (b2) immediately, because the last step of \cref{def:comphash} accumulates the
$\nhash{\cdot}$ values \emph{sorted}: if exchanging two members merely permutes the multiset
$\{\nhash{y}\}$, $\comphash$ is unchanged and only the assignment moves. (b2) needs the exchange to
change the multiset, which it does whenever it changes some $h_j$ --- and $h_j$ is computed in
$\HEnv$, so this is the typical case. \Cref{ex:ambiguous} exhibits both behaviours.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The side condition is decidable, and is computed]\label{cor:detect}
\src{abt:186-192, 210-217} Injectivity of $\phash{\cdot}$ on $V$ is decided by grouping the
members by permutation hash. \texttt{doHashCycle} does exactly this and emits
\texttt{IncompleteElementOrderingError}, listing the structurally equivalent sets.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Total function, conditional guarantee, aborting caller]\label{rem:notpartial}
It is tempting to summarize \cref{thm:ordering} as ``canonical ordering is a partial function'' and
``Unison refuses''. Both are slightly off, and the accurate version is more interesting.

$\comphash$ is a \emph{total} function: on the ambiguous input it returns a hash. What fails is not
definedness but \emph{presentation-independence} --- the property content addressing exists to
provide. The fallback is alphabetical order of user-chosen names, which is precisely the kind of
dependency the design is meant to eliminate.

The refusal is at the \emph{caller}. \texttt{doHashCycle} returns the condition as a warning, and its
own comment says ``hashing always succeeds even if it generates warnings'';
\texttt{crashOnHashingWarning} turns it into an exception, and the main term-component path takes that
route \src{ufile:284}, with a top-level handler rendering it as bug \texttt{E253299} plus a
workaround --- add a distinguishing binding such as \texttt{\_ = "this is the foo definition"}
\src{abt:41, 55-62; main:116}.

So for a top-level component the accurate statement is: \emph{a decidable side condition guards
presentation-independence, and the implementation aborts rather than store a hash that violates it.}
That is a stronger engineering position than a partial function --- no top-level component ever
receives a presentation-dependent identity --- and a sharper obligation for a formalism, because the
side condition has to appear in the statement of every downstream result about component identity. The
condition is tracked upstream as unisonweb/unison\#2787.

There is one caller for which none of that holds, and \cref{rem:innercycle} is it.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Inside a \texttt{let rec} the same condition is computed and discarded]\label{rem:innercycle}
\src{abt:169-174, 202-206} A recursive group nested inside a definition is hashed by the same
\texttt{doHashCycle}, reached through a different caller:
\begin{quote}\small
``We ignore incomplete element ordering warnings when calling in from \texttt{hash'}; we don't want to
error on that when hashing internal let-bindings.''
\end{quote}
The warning is bound to \texttt{\_warnings} and dropped. What is left is step 2 of
\cref{def:comphash}, whose sort is \emph{stable}: tied members keep their listing order, and that
order fixes their de Bruijn indices and so the enclosing definition's hash. For an inner cycle the
listing is not reset to name order the way top-level bindings are (\cref{def:comphash}, footnote), so
what survives into $\pi$ is the order the bindings were written.

It is observable, and the demonstration needs three definitions of the same function:
\[
\begin{array}{l@{\qquad}l@{\qquad}l}
  \texttt{m1 n = (a x = b x;\ b x = a x;\ a n)} & \texttt{\#2d5bs110vr} & \\
  \texttt{m2 n = (p x = q x;\ q x = p x;\ p n)} & \texttt{\#2d5bs110vr} & \just{members renamed} \\
  \texttt{m3 n = (b x = a x;\ a x = b x;\ a n)} & \texttt{\#qqn1r2c1od} & \just{members reordered}
\end{array}
\]
Renaming the two members changes nothing, which is \cref{thm:ualpha} behaving as advertised. Writing
them in the other order changes the identity of the definition that contains them, though no reader
could tell the two apart. Lifting the very same pair of bindings to the top level aborts with
\texttt{E253299}.

Two things follow. The guarantee of \cref{rem:notpartial} is a guarantee about top-level components
and not about hashing in general --- the system does silently produce a presentation-dependent
identity, one nesting level down. And the dependence there is on \emph{source order} rather than on
names, which is a different coordinate from \cref{thm:ordering}'s and arguably a worse one: a name is
at least something the user chose deliberately. The published workaround applies unchanged --- add a
distinguishing binding --- but nothing reports that it is needed.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Hashing collapses $\alpha$-equivalence and cycle permutation]\label{thm:ualpha}
Let $\phash{\cdot}$ be injective on $V$. Then $\comphash$ identifies two components exactly when they
are related by $\alpha$-equivalence of members (\cref{def:ualpha}) together with a permutation of the
cycle. It does not identify observationally equivalent members.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$(\Leftarrow)$ Binder names are discarded by \cref{def:uhash}, and permutation-independence is
\cref{thm:ordering}(a). $(\Rightarrow)$ By injectivity of $\mathsf{acc}$ on token streams and the
domain-separating tags of \cref{def:tokens}, downward through the structure.
\end{proof}

\section{Naming}\label{sec:unaming}

Names are not part of a definition, and nothing above consulted one. This section says what they are
attached to and what a namespace is, which is as much as \cref{sec:uupdate} needs; the object that
carries a namespace, and its history, are out of scope (\cref{sec:unotmodeled}).

\begin{definition}[Referent]\label{def:ureferent}
\src{referent:27-30} $\Referent ::= \URefT{r} \mid \UCon{r}{k}$. Names denote
\emph{referents}, not references: a name may denote a data constructor rather than a definition. With
no data declarations in scope (\cref{sec:unotmodeled}) the second arm is vestigial here, and is
retained only so that \cref{def:unames} has the right shape.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Namespace]\label{def:unames}
\src{branchh:22-39; sql:271} A namespace is a \emph{relation}
\[
  \UNames \;\subseteq\; \Name \times \Referent ,
\]
written $\UNames(x)$ for $\{\,\varrho \mid (x,\varrho) \in \UNames\,\}$. One name may denote several
referents and one referent may have several names; both are ordinary states rather than errors. The
derived view has the same shape --- names are recovered in memory as a \emph{relation} between
referents and names \src{branchtype:110-111}. The database agreed too, in the name-lookup index whose
key included the referent columns \src{sql:271}; that index was deleted in 2025 and the fragment is
now vestigial, which is itself the point of \cref{rem:conflict}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Unconflictedness]\label{def:unconf}
\src{branchtype:280-289} $\unconf{\UNames}{x}$ holds iff
$|\UNames(x)| = 1$, and $\unconf{\UNames}{}$ holds iff it holds at every name in $\dom(\UNames)$.
When it does, $\UNames$ determines a partial function and we write
$\uresolve{\UNames}{x}{\varrho}$. It is computed as a derived view, recomputed rather than
maintained --- and it is all-or-nothing: the implementation's \texttt{asUnconflicted} returns either
the whole unconflicted namespace or the offending name, never the unconflicted \emph{part} of a
namespace. So $\unconf{\UNames}{}$, not $\unconf{\UNames}{x}$, is the predicate operations actually
have available.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Conflict is representable; operations demand its absence]\label{rem:conflict}
Making conflict a legal state rather than an error at bind time is what lets a merge complete without
resolving everything first: the namespace can hold both candidates and the user can sort it out
afterwards. It is worth noting where the cost lands. \texttt{update} cannot proceed against a
conflicted namespace and \emph{asserts} unconflictedness before doing anything
\src{update:99-102}. And Unison's own forward-looking
\texttt{BranchV3} format removes conflicted names outright --- ``a V3 branch is a trimmed-down V2
branch: Names can't be conflicted'' \src{branchv3:16-30}. So the generality is
real, and the direction of travel appears to be away from it.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Namespace gestures]\label{def:gestures}
Besides \texttt{update} (\cref{def:update}), a user has three operations that change $\UNames$ and
nothing else. Writing $\varrho$ for the referent $\UNames$ binds at $x$:
\[
\begin{array}{l@{\qquad}l}
  \texttt{move}(x,y)   & \UNames' = \bigl(\UNames \setminus \{(x,\varrho)\}\bigr) \cup \{(y,\varrho)\} \\
  \texttt{alias}(x,y)  & \UNames' = \UNames \cup \{(y,\varrho)\} \\
  \texttt{delete}(x)   & \UNames' = \UNames \setminus \bigl(\{x\} \times \Referent\bigr)
\end{array}
\]
None of the three reads or writes $\UStore$, none can fail on a typing ground, and none changes any
hash. They are \texttt{move.term}, \texttt{alias.term} and \texttt{delete.term} in the interface.

Two preconditions are carried by the notation rather than by the equations. Writing $\varrho$ for
``the referent at $x$'' presupposes $\unconf{\UNames}{x}$. And the target of a \texttt{move} or an
\texttt{alias} must be unbound: the relation would hold the union perfectly well --- $y$ would simply
become conflicted --- but the commands refuse, with \emph{``A term named \texttt{bar} already
exists.''} So conflict remains representable and no gesture here can produce one, which is a third
instance of \cref{rem:conflict}'s pattern after \cref{def:unconf}'s all-or-nothing view and
\cref{def:update}'s precondition.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Renaming is free, and this is the usual reason given for content addressing]\label{rem:renamefree}
\Cref{def:gestures} is the formal content of the claim that a content-addressed codebase makes
renaming free. It is true and it is not a small thing: a rename is one row, it cannot break a caller,
it cannot invalidate a cached result (\cref{cor:wnoinval}), and no definition anywhere is rewritten,
because no definition anywhere contains the name. Nothing in \crefrange{sec:ustore}{sec:uupdate} can
even observe that one happened.

\Cref{sec:umerge} is where that stops being true. Merge is the one operation that reads names as
though they were content, and \cref{cor:noedit-user} is where the bill arrives.
\end{remark}

\section{The evaluation cache}\label{sec:ucache}

\begin{definition}[Watch cache]\label{def:watch}
\src{ctype:77-78; sql:121-127} The cache is a finite partial function
\[
  \UCache \;:\; (\Hash \times \Pos) \pfn \Term ,
\]
from the identity of an expression to its value. Two details are easy to get wrong and are worth
stating. The key is a \emph{reference}, not a bare hash --- the primary key is
$(\mathit{hash}, \mathit{component\ index})$, so a watch is addressed exactly the way
\cref{def:uref} addresses a definition. And the value is the evaluated result \emph{term}, stored
inline as a blob \src{sql:124}, not a hash into $\UStore$: nothing about caching a result puts that
result in the store.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[The key need not be a definition]\label{rem:watchkey}
$\dom(\UCache)$ identifies an \emph{expression}, which need not be named and need not be anything
the user declared --- the schema note says so outright: the key ``is an unevaluated term reference
\dots\ because the unevaluated term may not exist in the codebase: it is not added merely by watching
it without a name, e.g \texttt{> 2 + 3}'' \src{sql:141-143}. \Cref{prop:watchdom} is that sentence
as a statement about the model, and it is observable in a running system: after evaluating one watch,
the codebase's \texttt{hash} table has interned the watch's hash and its \texttt{hash\_object} table
has no row for it --- an identity with no object behind it. There is no notion of ``is a definition'' attached to stored data that the cache
would have to respect; a hash is a hash. This is what lets a watch expression in a scratch file be
cached on the same footing as a top-level binding.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Cache soundness is stable under growth]\label{thm:wstable}
If $\UCache(r) = v$ is sound in $\UStore$ and $\UStore \subseteq \UStore'$, it is sound in
$\UStore'$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Evaluating $r$ reads only entries reachable from $r$; all of them are in $\dom(\UStore)$ and, by
immutability, identical in $\UStore'$. The argument uses neither well-formedness nor anything about
the namespace, and is indifferent to whether the codomain is a hash or an inlined term.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why \cref{prop:watchdom} costs the cache nothing]\label{rem:watchdom-ok}
It is worth noticing what the proof above did \emph{not} use: that $r$ itself is in the store. It
used only that everything $r$ \emph{reads} is. A cached key that names nothing --- the situation
\cref{prop:watchdom} shows is reachable --- is therefore not an edge case the soundness argument has
to exclude; it is outside the argument's hypotheses altogether. That is the technical content of
``there is no notion of \emph{is a definition} attached to stored data'': had the cache been
specified as a map on definitions, this proposition would have been a bug report rather than an
observation.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[No invalidation is needed]\label{cor:wnoinval}
An entry, once written, is valid in every future store. Consequently no invalidation logic is
required --- and none exists; a search for one across the storage, codebase and CLI layers returns
nothing. Two removal paths exist and neither is invalidation: a debug-only wholesale
\texttt{clear\-Watches}, reachable as \texttt{debug.clear-cache}, and a garbage collection of
watches whose hash has no object, which deletes from the \texttt{watch} table rather than
\texttt{watch\_result} and whose only caller is a one-time schema migration.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[What ``never invalidated'' actually means]\label{rem:cachemeaning}
This is the claim Unison is best known for, and it is worth stating precisely because the informal
version invites a weaker reading. ``Caches are never invalidated'' is not an empirical observation
that definitions rarely change, and not a policy that could be traded away for freshness. It is a
consequence of two facts: the store is append-only, and the cache is keyed by a hash of exactly the
expression that was evaluated. Together they make staleness unrepresentable. Nothing in the argument
mentions names, types, or how often anyone edits anything.
\end{remark}

\section{Update}\label{sec:uupdate}

\subsection{Dependents}

\begin{definition}[Dependents]\label{def:dependents}
$\deps(h,i)$ is the set of references occurring in the $i$-th member of the component at $h$;
$\dependents$ is its converse, maintained as an index populated when a definition is saved. Note that
this index can exist because a reference remains a node in the stored representation even though it
hashes transparently at position $0$ (\cref{prop:transparent}): transparency is a property of the
hash, not of the representation.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Update scope]\label{def:uscope}
\src{ops:1125-1126; update:163-179; branchtype:280-289} Given a set $Q$ of references being updated
and a namespace $\UNames$,
\[
  \tdeps(Q) \;=\; \bigl(\dependents^{+}(Q) \;\cap\; \mathcal{S}\bigr) \setminus F
\]
where $\dependents^{+}$ is the transitive closure excluding self-references, $F$ is what the incoming
file itself shadows, and $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of references named in $\UNames$ once $\lib$ has
been removed. Note where the two side conditions actually sit. Excluding $\lib$ is not a filter
applied to the dependents; $\lib$ is deleted from the namespace \emph{before} $\mathcal{S}$ is read
off it. And unconflictedness is not a filter either: by \cref{def:unconf} the implementation cannot
take the unconflicted \emph{part} of a namespace, so $\mathcal{S}$ is well defined only because
\cref{def:update} has already demanded $\unconf{\UNames}{}$ of the whole non-$\lib$ namespace. A
separate rule, not part of $\tdeps$, rejects the update outright if the incoming file itself binds
anything under $\lib$ \src{update:139-144}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Naming is what puts a definition in scope]\label{rem:scopenaming}
An unnamed definition is not a dependent to be updated, even if it references the target. Naming is
the only thing that makes a stored object a participant in an edit --- which is a second place, after
\cref{rem:watchkey}, where the system declines to attach a ``definition'' status to stored data and
lets the namespace carry it instead.

Note also what is \emph{absent}: there is no type-based gating. Unison formerly classified each edit
as $\mathsf{Same} \mid \mathsf{Subtype} \mid \mathsf{Different}$ and propagated only the first two
automatically. The propagation machinery that consumed that classification was deleted in 2025; the
datatype itself survives, with no callers, because old history has to keep deserializing. The current
design computes the closure and lets the typechecker decide, reporting failure rather than predicting
it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Update, as rendering}

\begin{definition}[Pretty-print environment]\label{def:ppe}
\src{update:429-463} A $\ppe$ assigns each reference a name to
render it by, under two different suffixification strategies: definitions \emph{in the file} are
suffixified by name, definitions \emph{in the codebase} by hash. The asymmetry is forced --- aliases
sharing a suffix may be referred to by that suffix in the codebase but not in a file, where the parser
reports an ambiguity. The source gives the example: with \texttt{one.foo} and \texttt{two.foo} both in
the file, \texttt{hey = foo + foo} fails to parse.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Update]\label{def:update}
\src{update:89, 219-300} Given a typechecked incoming file $F$ and a
configuration $\UConfig{\UStore}{\UNames}{\UCache}$ with $\unconf{\UNames}{}$:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Let $Q$ be the references of the names $F$ redefines, and $D = \tdeps(Q)$.
  \item Render: $S = \render(\ppe, D)$, source text for every dependent.
  \item Parse and typecheck $F \cup S$ \emph{as one file}.
  \item If it typechecks, \textbf{store} each definition's component at the hash
        \cref{def:usave} already gave it, and rebind every affected name. This step is the
        \emph{commit}: by \cref{rem:saveident} the hashes are not computed here, they are merely
        acted on.
  \item If it does not, nothing is ingested; \textbf{remove from $\UNames$ every name $F$ binds and
        every name in $D$}, and deliver $F \cup S$ to the user as editable source on a dedicated
        update branch.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[How much step 5 removes]\label{rem:step5}
Two things about it are stronger than the natural reading. First, the removal is not confined to the
definitions that failed: the implementation subtracts, by name, \emph{everything the file binds}
together with \emph{every dependent it rendered}, whatever the typechecker's verdict on each. Second,
$F$'s own definitions do not survive either --- since nothing is ingested, the name the user was
trying to update is left denoting nothing at all, rather than still denoting its old referent.
\Cref{ex:update-fail} works this through, and it is the reason the operation is coherent: the
namespace is not left holding a mixture of old and new.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The failure case is the interesting one]\label{rem:residual}
Step 5 is the part of \texttt{update} least anticipated by informal descriptions of it. When
propagation fails, the namespace is not left holding broken definitions and the operation does not
roll back: the whole edit --- what the user wrote and everything it touched --- is \emph{evicted}
from the namespace, and its source becomes the user's working state. The namespace stays coherent by
construction, and the unfinished work is the scratch file. Note the dependency on \cref{rem:names} ---
what is handed back has to be readable, so binder names must have survived storage.

Neither ``commit'' nor ``roll back'' describes this. A rollback would restore the pre-edit namespace,
leaving the user's work only in whatever buffer they typed it into; a commit would leave broken
bindings. Step 5 does a third thing: it makes the namespace smaller and hands the difference to the
user as code. \Cref{rem:step5} records how much it removes, which is more than the name suggests.

The same closure computation feeds \texttt{merge}, which puts conflicted definitions and their
dependents into a scratch file together for the same reason: a conflict cannot be resolved in
isolation, because whatever the user picks, everything downstream must be re-typechecked against it.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Update propagates by exactly one strategy]\label{prop:follow-only}
The transition of \cref{def:update} admits no parameter controlling propagation: $\tdeps$ is fixed.
Every transitive dependent with an unconflicted name outside $\lib$ is rebuilt, and there is no way to
request that some callers be left pointing at the old definition. The nearest thing to that is to
choose a name nothing depends on, so that $\tdeps(Q)$ is empty --- abstention rather than an
operation, and not even a separate command: at this commit \texttt{add} is an alias for
\texttt{update} \src{inputpat:882-896}, the two having been merged in 2025. A per-definition choice does reappear at \emph{failure} time, since whatever
fails to typecheck comes back as source for the user to edit.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{The obligation update rests on}

\begin{proposition}[Update is correct only if printing inverts parsing]\label{prop:print-parse}
Let $d$ be a dependent not redefined by $F$, and let $d'$ be the definition obtained from step 4 for
$d$. The intended semantics of \cref{def:update} is that $d'$ differs from $d$ \emph{only} by having
its references to $Q$ replaced by their new targets. This holds iff, for every $d \in D$,
\[
  \uparse(\ppe,\ \render(\ppe, d)) \;=\; d
  \qquad\text{modulo exactly that replacement.}
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Step 3 reconstructs $d'$ from text alone. Any discrepancy introduced by the round trip --- a binder
renamed so as to change capture, an identifier resolving to a different alias, a different
associativity --- is preserved into $d'$ and changes its hash for a reason unrelated to the update.
Conversely, if the round trip is exact except at the references being retargeted, $d'$ is by
construction the intended substitution.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The property is understood precisely, and lives in a test]\label{rem:printparse}
The implementation's own comment for this step is: ``We are updating old references to new references
by rendering old references as names that are then parsed back to resolve to new references (the
world's weirdest implementation of AST substitution)''
\src{update:424-427}. The correctness property that comment
presupposes does not appear as a statement anywhere in the source. It is instead \emph{tested}:
\texttt{unison-src/transcripts-round-trip/} renders a regression corpus, re-adds it to a fresh
namespace, and asserts that the namespace diff is empty --- ``this diff should be empty if the two
namespaces are equivalent. If it's nonempty, the diff will show us the hashes that differ.'' The
corpus file is named \texttt{reparses-with-same-hash.u}, and its sibling \texttt{reparses.u} holds the
weaker cases that need only parse.

The naming is the point. The property is understood exactly, and the distinction between ``reparses''
and ``reparses with the same hash'' is drawn in the file names --- it is simply maintained as a golden
corpus rather than stated as an invariant. Given the size of Unison's surface language that is a
defensible engineering choice. \Cref{prop:print-parse} makes explicit what the corpus is protecting,
and the difficulty demonstrably concentrates in one place: the pretty-print environment of
\cref{def:ppe}.
\end{remark}

\section{Merge}\label{sec:umerge}

\texttt{update} rebuilds a namespace after one person edits it. \texttt{merge} reconciles two
namespaces edited independently. The two share a mechanism --- both work by rendering stored
definitions to source and parsing them back --- and therefore share the obligation of
\cref{prop:print-parse}. What merge adds is a decision \texttt{update} never has to make: given that
a definition differs on both branches, decide whether a \emph{human} changed it, or whether it
changed because something underneath it did. That decision is made by a second hash, computed from
names.

\subsection{Three namespaces, one store}

A branch is modeled here as a namespace. The causal history Unison attaches to one is out of scope
(\cref{sec:unotmodeled}), and so is computing the common ancestor of two branches. Merge is a
function of three namespaces in the sense of \cref{def:unames} --- $\NO$ (the common ancestor), $\NA$
(the branch being merged \emph{into}) and $\NB$ (the branch being merged \emph{from}) --- over one
store. The asymmetry of the last two is not incidental and is retained throughout: several steps
prefer $\NA$, and \cref{prop:merge-asym} is the consequence.

\begin{proposition}[The store never merges]\label{prop:merge-store}
Let $\UStore^{\mathsf{a}}$ and $\UStore^{\mathsf{b}}$ arise from independent sequences of ingests
(\cref{def:uingest}) starting from a common $\UStore^{\mathsf{o}}$. Then
$\UStore^{\mathsf{a}} \cup \UStore^{\mathsf{b}}$ is a store in the sense of \cref{def:ustore}, and
merge performs no reconciliation on it.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Well-definedness: if $h \in \dom(\UStore^{\mathsf{a}}) \cap \dom(\UStore^{\mathsf{b}})$ then both
components hash to $h$, so by \cref{thm:ualpha} they are the same component up to $\alpha$-equivalence
and cycle permutation, which is the identification $\comphash$ makes. No-dangling: each store is
closed under the references its components contain, and closure is preserved by union.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What that proposition is and is not]\label{rem:merge-store}
It is the formal content of the claim, often made for content-addressed codebases, that two people
cannot conflict in the code. The claim is true, and \cref{prop:merge-store} is why: the store is
append-only and keyed by content, so a union is always a store and never needs a decision.

It is also close to vacuous, and it is worth saying why rather than resting on it. Nothing a user
would call a merge conflict lives in $\UStore$. Conflicts live in $\UNames$, which is not content-addressed
--- it is a relation over names people choose. Everything in the rest of this section is about
$\UNames$, and none of it follows from \cref{prop:merge-store}.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Merge preconditions]\label{def:mergepre}
\src{merge2:208-222; mblob:98-102} Merge demands three things before it computes anything.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\roman*)]
  \item $\unconf{\NO}{}$, $\unconf{\NA}{}$ and $\unconf{\NB}{}$. By \cref{def:unconf} this is
        all-or-nothing on each of the three; a single conflicted name anywhere aborts the merge, and
        for $\NO$ the user cannot even act on the diagnosis.
  \item Neither $\NA$ nor $\NB$ binds anything under $\lib$, the same restriction
        \cref{def:uscope} imposes on \texttt{update}.
  \item No \emph{conflicted alias}: no pair of names that denote one referent in $\NO$ and different
        ones on a branch. If \texttt{foo} and \texttt{bar} were aliases and one branch left them
        denoting different things, merge stops.
\end{enumerate}
Condition (iii) is what makes the rest of the section well posed. Everything below indexes by name,
so a definition must not be able to be updated at one of its names and not at another.
\end{definition}

\subsection{The syntactic hash}

\begin{definition}[Naming function]\label{def:namer}
\src{mdiffblob:412-422; names:359-369} $\namer : \Referent \rightharpoonup \Name$ assigns each
referent one \emph{fully qualified} name, drawn from $\NA$ if $\NA$ names it at all, otherwise from
$\NB$, otherwise from $\NO$. The bias is by \emph{referent}, not by name: if $\NA$ has any name for
$r$, none of $\NB$'s names for $r$ are considered. Referents named nowhere are outside $\dom(\namer)$
and are treated below as denoting themselves.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Syntactic hash]\label{def:synhash}
\src{msynhash:237-246, 327-329, 386-390} For a resolved term $t$ (\cref{def:urterm}),
$\syn{\namer}{t}$ is computed by the recursion of \cref{def:uhash} with one substitution reversed: a
bound variable contributes its de Bruijn index, exactly as before, and a reference $r$ contributes
$\namer(r)$ as \emph{text} --- or, when $r \notin \dom(\namer)$, its hash. Binder names are discarded;
reference hashes are not consulted.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[The two hashes are duals, and only one is stored]\label{rem:syn-dual}
\Cref{def:uhash} erases names and keeps hashes. \Cref{def:synhash} erases hashes and keeps names.
They run over the same syntax with the same treatment of binders, and differ in exactly one clause.
The content hash is the definition's identity and is stored; the syntactic hash is computed during a
merge, used to make one decision, and discarded --- it appears in no table, and there is no operation
that retrieves a definition by it.

Two consequences follow directly from the definition. First, $\syn{\namer}{t}$ is a function of the
namespace as well as the term: the same stored definition has different syntactic hashes in different
namespaces. Second, equality of syntactic hashes is equality of \emph{rendered} form: two definitions
share a syntactic hash exactly when substituting $\namer$ through both yields the same syntax, which
is to say when a reader shown both under $\namer$ would see the same code. \Cref{def:mergediff} is
built entirely on that reading.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Propagation is invisible to the syntactic hash]\label{lem:prop-silent}
Let $d'$ be obtained from $d$ by replacing each reference $r$ with some $r'$ satisfying
$\namer(r) = \namer(r')$. Then $\syn{\namer}{d} = \syn{\namer}{d'}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Immediate: by \cref{def:synhash} a reference contributes only through $\namer$, and the two terms
agree everywhere else.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why that lemma is the whole idea]\label{rem:prop-silent}
\Cref{def:update} rebuilds every transitive dependent of an edit, so after any edit a great many
definitions have new hashes that no human typed. \Cref{lem:prop-silent} says those definitions are
nonetheless syntactically unchanged --- provided the edited definition kept its name, so that its old
and new versions have the same $\namer$-image. The implementation's own illustration is exactly this
hypothesis: with \texttt{foo = \#bar + 3} becoming \texttt{foo = \#bar2 + 3}, ``our pretty-print
environment has mapped both \texttt{\#bar} and \texttt{\#bar2} to the name \texttt{helper}, so each
version of \texttt{foo} would have the same syntactic hash. This indicates (to our merge algorithm)
that this was an auto-propagated update'' \src{msynhash:1-25}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The diff, and what a conflict is}

\begin{definition}[Branch diff]\label{def:mergediff}
\src{mnarrow:29-32; mdiff:52-84} For a branch $P \in \{\NA, \NB\}$ and a name $x$, the change
$\delta_P(x)$ is
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
  \dadd & x \notin \dom(\NO),\ x \in \dom(P) \\
  \ddel & x \in \dom(\NO),\ x \notin \dom(P) \\
  \dnil & \NO(x) = P(x) \\
  \dprop & \NO(x) \neq P(x) \text{ and } \syn{\namer}{\NO(x)} = \syn{\namer}{P(x)} \\
  \dupd & \NO(x) \neq P(x) \text{ and } \syn{\namer}{\NO(x)} \neq \syn{\namer}{P(x)}
\end{array}
\]
Only $\dadd$, $\ddel$ and $\dupd$ enter the diff. $\dnil$ is discarded before any hashing happens
--- equal referents have equal syntactic hashes, so the case is decided on the content hash alone
--- and $\dprop$ is separated out and set aside as an automatic propagation.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Only the syntactic hash decides]\label{rem:syn-only}
\src{msynhashed:9-26} The distinction between $\dprop$ and $\dupd$ is the only place in the algorithm
where the content hash of a definition is consulted at all, and even there it is consulted second: the
type carrying a definition through the diff pairs a referent with its syntactic hash, and its equality
instance compares \emph{only the syntactic hash} --- ``the \texttt{Eq} and \texttt{Ord} instances only
compares syntactic hashes''. Once two definitions' syntactic hashes differ, nothing downstream asks
what they actually are. A content-addressed system decides its merges by name.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Combination]\label{def:mergecombine}
\src{mtwodiff:24-40; mcombine:51-67} A name $x$ is \emph{conflicted} iff the pair
$(\delta_{\NA}(x), \delta_{\NB}(x))$ is $(\dadd,\dadd)$ or $(\dupd,\dupd)$ and, in addition,
$\syn{\namer}{\NA(x)} \neq \syn{\namer}{\NB(x)}$. Every other pair resolves:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Pair & Result \\
\midrule
one side only & that side's operation \\
$(\dadd,\dadd)$ or $(\dupd,\dupd)$, hashes equal & one operation, taking $\NA$'s referent \\
$(\ddel,\ddel)$ & delete \\
$(\ddel,\dupd)$ or $(\dupd,\ddel)$ & the update; the delete is discarded \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The merged namespace $\NM$ is $\NO$ with the resolved operations applied.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[A delete never wins]\label{rem:updel}
The fourth row is a deliberate choice and the source says so: ``These two are not a conflicts,
perhaps only temporarily, because it's easier to implement. We just ignore these deletes and keep the
updates'' \src{mcombine:60-63}. It matters more than it looks, because Unison has no rename
operation at the level of this diff: moving \texttt{foo} to \texttt{bar} appears as $\ddel$ at
\texttt{foo} and $\dadd$ at \texttt{bar}. So a rename racing an ordinary edit of the renamed
definition resolves by keeping both halves --- the new name from the rename, the old name from the
edit --- and \cref{ex:merge-dup} works that through.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Merge]\label{def:umerge}
\src{mblob:186-243; merge2:346-380} Given $\NO$, $\NA$, $\NB$ satisfying \cref{def:mergepre}:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Compute $\delta_{\NA}$, $\delta_{\NB}$ and the conflicted names (\cref{def:mergecombine}),
        and form $\NM$.
  \item Let $D$ be the conflicted definitions together with their transitive dependents in either
        branch --- the same closure \cref{def:uscope} computes, for the same reason: whatever
        resolves a conflict, everything downstream must be rechecked against it.
  \item Render $D$ to source under a naming function $\rnamer$, producing one file.
  \item If no name is conflicted, parse and typecheck that file against $\NM$. On success, ingest and
        bind; the merge is complete.
  \item Otherwise --- a conflicted name, or a file that does not parse or typecheck --- create a
        temporary branch holding $\NM$ and deliver the file to the user as editable source.
\end{enumerate}
$\rnamer$ is built like $\namer$ but suffixified, and prefers $\NA$'s names for the stated reason that
the result should ``look familiar to Alice (the one merging in Bob's changes)'' and should avoid
``superfluous textual conflicts that would arise from preferring Bob's names for Bob's code''
\src{mrender:93-104}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Two failures, one message]\label{rem:merge-twofail}
Step 5 covers two distinct situations that the implementation reports identically. Either a name is
conflicted, in which case the file contains two definitions under that name and cannot parse by
construction; or no name is conflicted and the file still fails, because rendering and re-parsing did
not round-trip. \Cref{prop:merge-print-parse} is about the second, and \cref{ex:merge-dangling}
exhibits it. The user sees the same sentence either way and must read the file to tell which happened.
\end{remark}

\subsection{What merge guarantees}

\begin{theorem}[A conflict requires two syntactic changes]\label{thm:noconflict}
If $\delta_{\NA}(x) \in \{\dnil, \dprop, \ddel\}$ then $x$ is not conflicted, whatever $\NB$ did.
Symmetrically for $\NB$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$\dnil$ and $\dprop$ do not enter the diff, so $x$ is absent from $\NA$'s side of the combination and
falls in the ``one side only'' row of \cref{def:mergecombine}. $\ddel$ pairs only as $(\ddel,\ddel)$
or $(\ddel,\dupd)$, neither of which is a conflict.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Propagation alone never conflicts]\label{cor:noedit}
Suppose $\NA$'s binding at $x$ was not authored on $\NA$: either $\NA(x) = \NO(x)$, or $\NA(x)$ is
obtained from $\NO(x)$ by replacing each reference $r$ with an $r'$ satisfying
$\namer(r) = \namer(r')$. Then $x$ is not conflicted.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The first case gives $\delta_{\NA}(x) = \dnil$; the second gives $\dprop$ by
\cref{lem:prop-silent}. Apply \cref{thm:noconflict}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Where the weight sits]\label{rem:cor-weight}
\Cref{cor:noedit} is the property one wants from a system that propagates edits automatically: if you
did not touch a definition, you cannot be asked to resolve a conflict in it. But its hypothesis is
about $\namer$, and no user has ever thought about $\namer$. What follows restates it as a condition
on what people did.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Authored; name-stable]\label{def:namestable}
Fix a branch $P$ reached from $\NO$ by a sequence of gestures --- each one an \texttt{update}
(\cref{def:update}) or a \texttt{move}, \texttt{alias} or \texttt{delete} (\cref{def:gestures}).
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item A name $x$ is \textbf{authored on $P$} if some \texttt{update} in the sequence submitted a
        file binding $x$. Names that changed only because \texttt{update} rebuilt their dependents
        are not authored.
  \item $P$ is \textbf{name-stable} if no gesture in the sequence is a \texttt{move}, an
        \texttt{alias} or a \texttt{delete}, and no \texttt{update} in it binds a name to a
        definition that already has one. The last clause is not redundant with \texttt{alias}: by
        \cref{thm:ualpha}, writing out a definition that already exists produces the same referent,
        so a duplicate is an alias arrived at without asking for one.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{corollary}[Nobody is asked about code nobody wrote, if nothing is renamed]\label{cor:noedit-user}
If $\NA$ and $\NB$ are both name-stable, then no name authored on neither branch is conflicted.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $x$ be authored on neither. If $x \notin \dom(\NO)$ then, since neither branch moves or aliases,
$x$ is bound on a branch only if authored there, so $x$ is in neither $\NA$ nor $\NB$ and does not
appear in the diff. Otherwise $x \in \dom(\NO)$, and by \cref{thm:noconflict} it is enough to show
$\delta_{\NA}(x) \neq \dupd$; by \cref{cor:noedit} it is enough to show $\namer(r) = \namer(r')$
for each reference $r$ of $\NO(x)$ that \cref{def:update} retargeted to $r'$.

Let $y$ be the name at which that dependency sits, so $\NO(y) = r$ and $\NA(y) = r'$. Since $\NA$ is
name-stable, $y$ is $\NA$'s only name for $r'$, so $\namer(r') = y$. And $\NA$ has no name for $r$ at
all: $y$ now denotes $r'$, and no alias or duplicate gave $r$ another. So $\namer(r)$ falls through to
$\NB$, which by its own name-stability names $r$ either at $y$ or nowhere --- at $y$ if it left the
dependency alone, nowhere if it updated it, in which case $\namer(r)$ falls through again to $\NO$,
where $r$ is named $y$. Either way $\namer(r) = y = \namer(r')$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Which is to say: only renaming does this]\label{rem:onlyrenames}
Read contrapositively, \cref{cor:noedit-user} says that the \emph{only} user action that can put
someone in front of a conflict in a definition they never opened is a name moving. Adding, updating
and deleting are all safe, at any depth of the dependency graph and in any combination --- which is
the property one wanted, and it holds. \Cref{def:gestures} is the exception, and it is the one class
of gesture that costs nothing anywhere else in the system.

Three details of the hypothesis are worth drawing out.

\emph{It is required of both branches, not just the one that did not edit.} If $\NA$ updates a
dependency in place and $\NB$ renames it, then $\namer$ takes $\NB$'s new name for the old version and
$\NA$'s unchanged name for the new one, and $\NA$'s untouched dependents are classified as authored.
$\NA$ can be perfectly disciplined and still be asked about code it never wrote.

\emph{An alias is enough; a \texttt{move} is not needed.} $\namer$ chooses among a referent's names by
a fixed priority --- relative before absolute, then fewest name segments \src{ppenames:141-151} ---
so aliasing an updated definition to a shorter name changes which name it renders under, with the same
effect as renaming it.

\emph{Unconflicted is not the same as unseen.} Step 2 of \cref{def:umerge} pulls the dependents of
every conflict into the same file, so a definition can appear in front of the user without being
conflicted. What \cref{cor:noedit-user} rules out is being asked to \emph{choose}.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Renaming defeats the side condition]\label{prop:rename-conflict}
There are configurations in which no definition at $x$ was authored on either branch beyond one
party's ordinary edit, and $x$ is nonetheless conflicted. It suffices that $\NA$ update a definition
$y$ on which $x$ depends and change $y$'s name in the same operation, and that $\NB$ edit $x$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\NO$ bind $y \mapsto r$ and $x \mapsto d$ with $r$ occurring in $d$. Let $\NA$ bind
$y' \mapsto r'$ and $x \mapsto d'$, where $d'$ is $d$ with $r$ replaced by $r'$ --- the propagation
\cref{def:update} performs. Since $\NA$ has no name for $r$ and $\NB$ does, $\namer(r)$ is $y$; since
$\NA$ names $r'$, $\namer(r') = y'$. So $\syn{\namer}{d} \neq \syn{\namer}{d'}$ and
$\delta_{\NA}(x) = \dupd$. If $\NB$ edits $x$ then $\delta_{\NB}(x) = \dupd$ with a different
syntactic hash, and $x$ is conflicted by \cref{def:mergecombine}. \Cref{ex:merge-rename} is this
configuration with three definitions.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Renaming and editing are two gestures and one intention]\label{rem:rename-gesture}
The hypothesis of \cref{prop:rename-conflict} is not exotic. Renaming is \texttt{move}
(\cref{def:gestures}) and editing is \texttt{update}, so they are two commands --- but ``this is
badly named and also wrong'' is one thought, and it is discharged by running them back to back. That
is all \cref{ex:merge-rename} does.

A \texttt{move} with no \texttt{update} is a different matter and does \emph{not} produce a conflict:
$\namer$ follows the referent, so the ancestor's version of $x$ renders under the new name too and the
two syntactic hashes agree. \Cref{ex:merge-dangling} shows what it produces instead, and
\cref{ex:merge-dup} what happens when the other branch edits the definition being renamed. The three
are disjoint, and \cref{cor:noedit-user} fails in all three.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[The side condition is computed, and discarded]\label{cor:unchecked}
\src{mrename:31-96; mdiffblob:171-177} Merge builds, for each definition, the set of names it gained
and the set it lost between $\NO$ and each branch, and narrows that to the one-to-one cases --- the
renames. At \texttt{db60ce2} the result is passed to a debug printer and to nothing else. No part of
the diff, the combination, $\NM$ or the rendered file consults it.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Compare \cref{cor:detect}]\label{rem:two-side-conditions}
This document now has two conditional guarantees. \Cref{thm:ordering} holds unless two members of a
cycle are indistinguishable; that condition is decided at ingest and the implementation aborts rather
than proceed (\cref{cor:detect}, \cref{rem:notpartial}). \Cref{cor:noedit} holds unless a name moved;
that condition is computed and discarded, and merge proceeds either way, reporting a conflict it
cannot distinguish from a real one.

The two are not equally tractable. The rename detection of \cref{cor:unchecked} groups definitions by
\emph{syntactic hash}, so it finds a name that moved while the definition stayed put --- and that is
the case \cref{rem:rename-gesture} says does not produce a conflict. The case that does produce one
is a rename \emph{and} an edit, where the syntactic hash changes too and the two versions are not
grouped. So the machinery that exists addresses the harmless case; the harmful case needs a signal
that a diff of two namespaces does not contain.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The one place the condition is repaired]\label{rem:libcanon}
\src{mdiffblob:463-505} There is an exception, and it is instructive. Before $\namer$ is built, names
under $\lib$ have a trailing mangled version number stripped, so that \texttt{lib.base\_1\_0\_0.foo}
and \texttt{lib.base\_2\_0\_0.foo} receive the same name. The purpose is stated: it is done ``to make
it more likely that we classify things that are \emph{probably} true propagated updates as such'',
and it is described in the source as a hack.

It is the hypothesis of \cref{cor:noedit} being restored by hand. Upgrading a library renames
everything in it, so without this every definition that mentions the library would be classified as
authored, and every concurrent edit would conflict. Rather than track the rename, merge forces the old
and new names to be equal. That works for the one renaming scheme whose shape is known in advance.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Merge is not commutative]\label{prop:merge-asym}
Merging $\NB$ into $\NA$ and merging $\NA$ into $\NB$ can yield different outcomes, including one
succeeding where the other fails, with no conflict reported in either direction.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Two mechanisms suffice independently. $\namer$ and $\rnamer$ both prefer $\NA$'s names by referent
(\cref{def:namer}), so both the classification of \cref{def:mergediff} and the text produced by
\cref{def:umerge} depend on which branch is the target. And the second row of
\cref{def:mergecombine} takes $\NA$'s referent when two syntactic hashes agree. \Cref{ex:merge-dangling}
exhibits a pair of branches for which one direction is a clean merge and the other is a failure.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Merge's round trip spans two namespaces]\label{prop:merge-print-parse}
Step 4 of \cref{def:umerge} requires, for every definition $d$ it renders,
\[
  \uparse(\NM,\ \render(\rnamer, d)) \;=\; d \qquad\text{modulo the intended retargeting,}
\]
which is \cref{prop:print-parse} with two differences. The environment that renders is not the
environment that parses: $\rnamer$ prefers $\NA$'s names, while $\NM$ has applied both branches'
deletions and additions. And nothing requires that a name $\rnamer$ emits still exists in $\NM$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{remark}[The gap is reachable]\label{rem:merge-gap}
If $\NB$ renames a definition and $\NA$ does not, then $\NM$ contains $\NB$'s new name and not the old
one --- the rename is an unconflicted delete and an unconflicted add, so both halves are applied. But
$\rnamer$ prefers $\NA$'s names, and $\NA$ still calls that definition by the old one. The file is
therefore written using a name the namespace it will be parsed against no longer has, and step 4
fails. \Cref{ex:merge-dangling} is this, in three definitions. Reversing the roles removes the
mismatch, which is \cref{prop:merge-asym}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[What is not claimed]\label{rem:merge-soundness}
\Cref{thm:noconflict} and its corollary bound \emph{spurious} conflicts. The dual question --- whether
every real disagreement is reported --- is left open here. The case to examine is the second row of
\cref{def:mergecombine}: when both branches update $x$ to definitions with equal syntactic hashes but
different referents, merge takes $\NA$'s silently. That is defensible only if the disagreement
underneath is reported at some other name, and while we expect it is, we have not established it.
\end{remark}

\section{Worked examples}\label{sec:examples}

Each example is a derivation: every line applies an operation defined above, with the definition
named on the right. Symbolic hashes are written \texttt{\#a}, \texttt{\#b}; $\ulcorner t \urcorner$
abbreviates $\uhash{\HEnv}{t}$ where the environment is clear; and $r_{+}$ abbreviates
$\UBuiltin{\texttt{Nat.+}}$, a builtin, which therefore never acquires a hash of its own.

\subsection{Ingesting one definition}\label{ex:ingest}

\[
\begin{array}{r@{\;}l@{\quad}l}
  e &= \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Nat.+}}}{\uvar{n}}}{\unat{1}}}
    & \just{source term, \cref{def:uterm}} \\[3pt]
  \Res{\{\texttt{inc}\}}{\UNames}(e)
    &= \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uapp{\uref{r_{+}}}{\uvar{n}}}{\unat{1}}}
    & \just{resolution, \cref{def:uresolution}} \\[3pt]
  {} &\utypes{\emptyset}{\Res{\{\texttt{inc}\}}{\UNames}(e)}{\uarr{\TNat}{\TNat}}
    & \just{typing, \cref{def:utyping}} \\[3pt]
  t &= \uann{\Res{\{\texttt{inc}\}}{\UNames}(e)}{\uarr{\TNat}{\TNat}}
    & \just{type incorporation, \cref{def:typeinhash}} \\[3pt]
  \bg &= \{\,\texttt{inc} \mapsto t\,\}
    & \just{binding group, \cref{def:bindgroup}} \\[3pt]
  C &= \langle(\texttt{inc}, t)\rangle,\quad k = 1
    & \just{one SCC, \cref{def:uingest}} \\[6pt]
  \phash{\texttt{inc}} &= \uhash{\mathsf{L}(\{\texttt{inc}\})}{t}
    & \just{step 1, \cref{def:comphash}} \\[3pt]
  \HEnv &= \mathsf{R}(\texttt{inc}),\qquad h_1 = \uhash{\HEnv}{t}
    & \just{step 2} \\[3pt]
  \nhash{\texttt{inc}} &= \mathsf{acc}[\mathsf{Tag}\,1,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\,h_1,\
                          \mathsf{Hashed}\,\uhash{\HEnv}{\uvar{\texttt{inc}}}]
    & \just{step 3} \\[3pt]
  \texttt{\#a} &= \comphash(C) = \mathsf{acc}[\mathsf{Hashed}\,\nhash{\texttt{inc}}]
    & \just{step 3} \\[6pt]
  \UStore' &= \upd{\UStore}{\texttt{\#a}}{\langle t \rangle}
    & \just{store, \cref{def:ustore}} \\[3pt]
  \UNames' &= \UNames \cup \{(\texttt{inc},\ \URefT{\UDer{\texttt{\#a}}{0}})\}
    & \just{naming, \cref{def:unames}}
\end{array}
\]

The name is attached last, to a definition that was complete without it. And note the two
accumulations in the final three lines: $\comphash(C) \neq h_1$ even at $k = 1$, which
\cref{ex:transparency} turns on.

\subsection{An alias, and what transparency gives}\label{ex:transparency}

\[
\begin{array}{r@{\;}l@{\quad}l}
  e_x &= \uapp{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Nat.+}}}{\unat{1}}}{\unat{1}},
  \qquad e_y = \uvar{\texttt{x}}
    & \just{source, \cref{def:uterm}} \\[3pt]
  \Res{\{\texttt{x},\texttt{y}\}}{\UNames}(e_y) &= \uvar{\texttt{x}}
    & \just{unresolved: $\texttt{x} \in B$, \cref{def:uresolution}} \\[3pt]
  \bg &= \{\,\texttt{x} \mapsto X,\ \ \texttt{y} \mapsto \uann{\uvar{\texttt{x}}}{\TNat}\,\}
    & \just{\cref{def:bindgroup}} \\[3pt]
  X &= \uann{(\uapp{\uapp{\uref{r_{+}}}{\unat 1}}{\unat 1})}{\TNat} & \\[3pt]
  C_1 &= \langle(\texttt{x}, X)\rangle,\qquad C_2 = \langle(\texttt{y}, \cdot)\rangle
    & \just{two SCCs, \cref{def:uingest}} \\[6pt]
  \texttt{\#x} &= \comphash(C_1)
    & \just{as in \cref{ex:ingest}} \\[3pt]
  Y &= \uann{\uref{\UDer{\texttt{\#x}}{0}}}{\TNat}
    & \just{substitution before $C_2$, \cref{def:uingest}} \\[6pt]
  \uhashc{\uref{\UDer{\texttt{\#x}}{0}}} &= \texttt{\#x}
    & \just{\cref{prop:transparent}} \\[3pt]
  \uhashc{Y} &= \mathsf{acc}[\ldots,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\,\texttt{\#x},\ \ldots]
    & \just{via \cref{prop:transparent}} \\[3pt]
  \uhashc{X} &= \mathsf{acc}[\ldots,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\,\uhashc{B_x},\ \ldots]
    & \just{same position} \\[3pt]
  B_x &= \uapp{\uapp{\uref{r_{+}}}{\unat 1}}{\unat 1} &
\end{array}
\]

\begin{remark}[The usual summary of transparency is too strong]\label{rem:transparency-limit}
The source comment illustrates transparency with exactly this pair, saying that \texttt{x = 1 + 1}
and \texttt{y = x} ``hash the same''. Comparing the last two lines, that would require
\[
  \uhashc{\uapp{\uapp{\uref{r_{+}}}{\unat 1}}{\unat 1}} \;=\; \texttt{\#x} ,
\]
the referent's \emph{term} hash to equal its \emph{component} hash. But \texttt{\#x} is built
\emph{from} $\uhashc{X}$ by the two accumulations at the end of \cref{ex:ingest}, so the equation
asks the hash function for a fixed point of a particular shape. Transparency passes along the
component hash; inlining would contribute the term hash; \cref{def:comphash} puts two layers between
them. So the two definitions receive different hashes, and inlining a reference into a larger term
does not preserve that term's hash either.

What \emph{is} true is what \cref{prop:transparent} says: a position-$0$ reference is
indistinguishable from any term hashing to \texttt{\#x}. The comment's own caveat is precisely this
condition --- ``so long as \texttt{Reference.Derived} ctors are created using the same hashing
function as is used here''. There is a construction satisfying it, \texttt{hashClosedTerm}, which
builds $\UDer{\uhashc{t}}{0}$ directly from a term's hash \src{term:124-125}; references made that
way \emph{are} interchangeable with their referents. The component construction is not that way.

We flag this as the place we are least confident of our reading, since it bears on a
frequently-repeated description of the system. If the definition-level claim does hold, we have
misread the relationship between $\comphash$ and the member hashes.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A recursive group}\label{ex:cycle}

Write $\langle j \rangle$ for the hash of de Bruijn index $j$, and $V = \{\texttt{cd},\texttt{cu}\}$.

\[
\begin{array}{r@{\;}l@{\quad}l}
  e_{\texttt{cd}} &= \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{cu}}}{\uvar{n}}},
  \qquad
  e_{\texttt{cu}} = \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{cd}}}{(\uapp{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Nat.+}}}{\uvar n}}{\unat 1})}}
    & \just{source} \\[3pt]
  \Res{V}{\UNames}(e_{\texttt{cd}}) &= e_{\texttt{cd}}
    & \just{both names in $B$, \cref{def:uresolution}} \\[3pt]
  C &= \langle(\texttt{cd}, \cdot), (\texttt{cu}, \cdot)\rangle,\quad k = 2
    & \just{one SCC, \cref{def:uingest}} \\[6pt]
  \phash{\texttt{cd}} &= \uhash{\mathsf{L}(V)}{\ulam{n}{\uapp{\langle 1\rangle}{\langle 0\rangle}}}
    & \just{step 1, \cref{def:comphash}} \\[3pt]
  \phash{\texttt{cu}} &= \uhash{\mathsf{L}(V)}{\ulam{n}{\uapp{\langle 1\rangle}{(\uapp{\uapp{\uref{r_{+}}}{\langle 0\rangle}}{\unat 1})}}}
    & \just{step 1} \\[3pt]
  \phash{\texttt{cd}} &\neq \phash{\texttt{cu}}
    & \just{the bodies differ} \\[3pt]
  \HEnv &= \mathsf{R}(\texttt{cd})\,\mathsf{R}(\texttt{cu})
    & \just{step 2, sorted by $\phash{\cdot}$} \\[3pt]
  \sigma &= \text{sort by } \nhash{\cdot}
    & \just{step 3} \\[3pt]
  \texttt{\#c} &= \comphash(C)
    & \just{step 3}
\end{array}
\]

Neither name enters $\phash{\cdot}$, so the ordering is decided by structure alone --- which is what
\cref{thm:ordering}(a) requires. The second sort $\sigma$ is by a hash of the first sort's index and
need not agree with it (\cref{rem:twoorders}), so \texttt{cu} may take position $0$. Whichever member
does \emph{is referenced transparently, and the other opaquely} (\cref{prop:transparent}) --- an
asymmetry between two definitions the user wrote symmetrically, and cannot observe.

\subsection{When the canonical order is ambiguous}\label{ex:ambiguous}

The two behaviours \cref{thm:ordering}(b1) and (b2) separate here have been computed, not only
derived; see \cref{sec:paths}.

Change the two so they differ only by which of the other they call:

\[
\begin{array}{r@{\;}l@{\quad}l}
  e_{\texttt{ping}} &= \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{pong}}}{\uvar{n}}},
  \qquad
  e_{\texttt{pong}} = \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{ping}}}{\uvar{n}}}
    & \just{source} \\[3pt]
  \phash{\texttt{ping}} &= \uhash{\mathsf{L}(V)}{\ulam{n}{\uapp{\langle 1\rangle}{\langle 0\rangle}}}
    & \just{step 1, \cref{def:comphash}} \\[3pt]
  \phash{\texttt{pong}} &= \uhash{\mathsf{L}(V)}{\ulam{n}{\uapp{\langle 1\rangle}{\langle 0\rangle}}}
    & \just{step 1} \\[3pt]
  \phash{\texttt{ping}} &= \phash{\texttt{pong}}
    & \just{$\phash{\cdot}$ not injective} \\[3pt]
  \HEnv &= \mathsf{R}(\texttt{ping})\,\mathsf{R}(\texttt{pong})
    & \just{stable sort $\Rightarrow$ name order}
\end{array}
\]

In $\mathsf{L}(V)$ both mutual references collapse to one index, so the two bodies become the
\emph{same} term and the sort has nothing to separate them. It is stable, and the list arrived in
name order, so \texttt{ping} precedes \texttt{pong} \textbf{for no reason but the alphabet}.

Renaming \texttt{ping} to \texttt{zing} swaps the binding order --- and here it is worth being
careful, because the obvious conclusion is wrong. The two bodies are $\ulam{n}{\uapp{\langle
1\rangle}{\langle 0\rangle}}$ under either listing, so the swap does not change the multiset
$\{h_1,h_2\}$; it exchanges which name owns which index, hence which $\nhash{\cdot}$, and
\cref{def:comphash} accumulates those \emph{sorted}. So \texttt{\#c} is unchanged and what moves is
\textbf{which member holds position $0$} --- which one is referenced transparently and which
opaquely. That is \cref{thm:ordering}(b1), and it is the consequence that always holds.

For (b2) --- $\comphash$ itself moving --- the exchange has to change a member hash rather than
merely relabel it. Two bodies that mention the cycle asymmetrically do that:
\[
  \texttt{foo} = \uapp{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Nat.+}}}{(\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{foo}}}{\unat 0})}}{(\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{bar}}}{\unat 0})},
  \qquad
  \texttt{bar} = \uapp{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Nat.+}}}{(\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{foo}}}{\unat 0})}}{(\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{bar}}}{\unat 0})}
\]
are tied under $\mathsf{L}(V)$ but, in $\HEnv$, hash to a term mentioning $\langle 1 \rangle$ before
$\langle 0 \rangle$ or the reverse depending on which name took the lower index --- so renaming
\texttt{bar} to \texttt{zar} changes $h_1$ and $h_2$, hence the shared token prefix
$\overline{\tau}$, hence \texttt{\#c}.

Either way the implementation detects the tie (\cref{cor:detect}) and aborts rather than store a
name-dependent identity, suggesting the user add something like
$\texttt{\_} = \utext{this is the ping definition}$ --- which is a request to make $\phash{\cdot}$
injective again. Note that it aborts on the tie, not on (b2): it declines the whole class rather than
trying to tell the harmless case from the harmful one, which \cref{rem:notpartial} argues is the
right call.

\subsection{An update that succeeds}\label{ex:update-ok}

Starting state, from two earlier ingests:
$\texttt{scale} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#s}}{0}$ and $\texttt{double} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#d}}{0}$,
where \texttt{double}'s stored body is $\ulam{n}{\uapp{\uref{\UDer{\texttt{\#s}}{0}}}{\uvar n}}$. The
user submits a new \texttt{scale} of the same type, $\uarr{\TNat}{\TNat}$.

\[
\begin{array}{r@{\;}l@{\quad}l}
  Q &= \{\UDer{\texttt{\#s}}{0}\}
    & \just{what $F$ redefines, \cref{def:update}} \\[3pt]
  \tdeps(Q) &= \{\UDer{\texttt{\#d}}{0}\}
    & \just{\cref{def:uscope}: named, unconflicted, not in $\lib$} \\[3pt]
  S &= \render(\ppe, \tdeps(Q))
    & \just{\cref{def:ppe}} \\[3pt]
  {} &= ``\texttt{double = }\ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{scale}}}{\uvar n}}\text{''}
    & \just{a \emph{name}, not a hash} \\[3pt]
  \uparse(\ppe, S) &\rightsquigarrow \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{scale}}}{\uvar n}}
    & \just{a \emph{variable} again, \cref{def:uterm}} \\[3pt]
  \Res{\emptyset}{\UNames}(\cdot) &= \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uref{\UDer{\texttt{\#s}'}{0}}}{\uvar n}}
    & \just{\emph{here} the reference is retargeted} \\[3pt]
  {} &\utypes{\emptyset}{F \cup S}{\ } \text{ succeeds}
    & \just{\cref{def:utyping}} \\[3pt]
  \texttt{\#s}',\ \texttt{\#d}' &= \hashcomps(\bg_{F \cup S})
    & \just{\cref{def:uingest}} \\[3pt]
  \UNames'' &= \UNames[\texttt{scale} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#s}'}{0}]
    & \just{\cref{def:update}, step 4} \\[3pt]
  \UNames''' &= \UNames''[\texttt{double} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#d}'}{0}] &
\end{array}
\]

The fifth line is the whole mechanism: retargeting happens because a printed \emph{name} resolves
against the new namespace. \texttt{double}'s source text is character-for-character what it was, and
its hash changed anyway. Meanwhile \texttt{\#d} is still in $\UStore$, still referring to
\texttt{\#s}, still meaning what it meant --- so any cached result for it remains valid
(\cref{cor:wnoinval}).

\subsection{An update that fails}\label{ex:update-fail}

Same starting state; this time the new \texttt{scale} is
$\ulam{s}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Text.size}}}{\uvar s}}$, of type $\uarr{\TText}{\TNat}$.

\[
\begin{array}{r@{\;}l@{\quad}l}
  Q,\ \tdeps(Q),\ S &\text{ as before}
    & \just{\cref{def:uscope,def:ppe}} \\[3pt]
  {} &\utypes{\emptyset}{F \cup S}{\ } \text{ fails at \texttt{double}}
    & \just{$n : \TNat$ against $\uarr{\TText}{\TNat}$} \\[3pt]
  \UStore' &= \UStore
    & \just{nothing is ingested, \cref{rem:step5}} \\[3pt]
  \UNames'' &= \UNames \setminus \{(\texttt{scale}, \cdot),\ (\texttt{double}, \cdot)\}
    & \just{\cref{def:update}, step 5} \\[3pt]
  \text{delivered} &= F \cup S
    & \just{step 5}
\end{array}
\]

Note what the second line does \emph{not} say. \texttt{scale} is not rebound to a new hash, and it is
not left denoting its old one: it is among the names $F$ binds, so step 5 removes it, and since
nothing was ingested there is no \texttt{\#s}$'$ for it to denote. After a failed update the
namespace contains neither name.

The namespace is left coherent: it contains no name denoting a definition that fails to typecheck,
and no half-applied edit. \texttt{\#s} and \texttt{\#d} have not been deleted or altered --- they
have stopped being reachable by name, which is the only kind of deletion the store supports --- and
the unfinished work is a file, containing both the user's new \texttt{scale} and the \texttt{double}
it broke. \Cref{rem:residual} discusses why this is the design's most distinctive feature, and
\cref{prop:print-parse} is the obligation the third line rests on: $\render$ must produce something
$\uparse$ turns back into \texttt{double}, differing only in what \texttt{scale} resolves to.

\subsection{Three merges from one ancestor}\label{ex:merge-setup}

The next three examples share a common ancestor with two definitions, a formatter and its one caller:
\[
  \NO = \{\,\texttt{fmt} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#f}}{0},\quad
           \texttt{show} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#c}}{0}\,\}
  \qquad
  \UStore(\texttt{\#c})_0 = \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uref{\UDer{\texttt{\#f}}{0}}}{\uvar n}}
\]
with $\texttt{fmt} : \uarr{\TNat}{\TText}$. In each example one branch is the merge target $\NA$ and
the other the source $\NB$; nobody edits a definition twice, and every branch is the result of a
single \texttt{update}. The three differ only in who does what.

\subsection{A rename manufactures a conflict}\label{ex:merge-rename}

$\NA$ rewrites the formatter and renames it in the same edit; \cref{def:update} propagates to
\texttt{show}, which $\NA$ never opens. $\NB$ edits \texttt{show} and leaves the formatter alone.
\[
  \NA = \{\texttt{price} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#f}'}{0},\ \texttt{show} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#c}'}{0}\}
  \qquad
  \NB = \{\texttt{fmt} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#f}}{0},\ \texttt{show} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#c}''}{0}\}
\]
\[
\begin{array}{r@{\;}l@{\quad}l}
  \namer(\UDer{\texttt{\#f}'}{0}) &= \texttt{price}
    & \just{$\NA$ names it, \cref{def:namer}} \\[3pt]
  \namer(\UDer{\texttt{\#f}}{0}) &= \texttt{fmt}
    & \just{$\NA$ does not; $\NB$ does} \\[3pt]
  \syn{\namer}{\NO(\texttt{show})} &= \syn{\namer}{\ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{fmt}}}{\uvar n}}} & \\[3pt]
  \syn{\namer}{\NA(\texttt{show})} &= \syn{\namer}{\ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{price}}}{\uvar n}}}
    & \just{same code, one identifier} \\[3pt]
  \delta_{\NA}(\texttt{show}) &= \dupd
    & \just{\cref{def:mergediff}: the two differ} \\[3pt]
  \delta_{\NB}(\texttt{show}) &= \dupd & \\[3pt]
  \texttt{show} &\text{ is conflicted}
    & \just{\cref{def:mergecombine}}
\end{array}
\]
The fourth line is the whole failure. $\NA$'s \texttt{show} is the ancestor's own definition with one
identifier substituted by propagation, and it is presented to the user as one side of a conflict to be
resolved against $\NB$'s. Two further consequences are worth noting. $\delta_{\NA}(\texttt{fmt}) = \ddel$
resolves unopposed, so $\NM$ has no \texttt{fmt} --- and $\NB$'s half of the conflict, which the user
must now edit, refers to \texttt{fmt}. Whichever side the user keeps, the rename has to be applied by
hand, which is the work \cref{prop:merge-store} was supposed to have removed.

\subsection{A rename alone: no conflict, and a merge that still fails}\label{ex:merge-dangling}

Now the rename carries no edit. $\NB$ renames the formatter and stops; $\NA$ edits \texttt{show}.
\[
  \NA = \{\texttt{fmt} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#f}}{0},\ \texttt{show} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#c}'}{0}\}
  \qquad
  \NB = \{\texttt{price} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#f}}{0},\ \texttt{show} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#c}}{0}\}
\]
\[
\begin{array}{r@{\;}l@{\quad}l}
  \delta_{\NA} &= \{\texttt{show} \mapsto \dupd\}
    & \just{$\texttt{fmt}$ unchanged, so $\dnil$} \\[3pt]
  \delta_{\NB} &= \{\texttt{fmt} \mapsto \ddel,\ \texttt{price} \mapsto \dadd\}
    & \just{a rename is a delete and an add} \\[3pt]
  \text{conflicted} &= \emptyset
    & \just{no name appears on both sides} \\[3pt]
  \NM &= \{\texttt{price} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#f}}{0},\ \texttt{show} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#c}'}{0}\} & \\[3pt]
  \rnamer(\UDer{\texttt{\#f}}{0}) &= \texttt{fmt}
    & \just{$\rnamer$ prefers $\NA$, \cref{def:umerge}} \\[3pt]
  \render(\rnamer, \NM(\texttt{show})) &= ``\texttt{show = }\ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{fmt}}}{\uvar n}}\text{''} & \\[3pt]
  \uparse(\NM, \cdot) &\text{ fails}
    & \just{$\texttt{fmt} \notin \dom(\NM)$}
\end{array}
\]
No name is conflicted and the merge fails anyway, at step 4 of \cref{def:umerge} rather than step 1.
The file handed back contains a single \texttt{show} --- not the two-sided form a conflict produces
--- written in terms of a name the branch it was written for does not have.

Exchanging the roles fixes it. With $\NB$ as the target, $\rnamer$ prefers \texttt{price}, the render
agrees with $\NM$, and the merge completes cleanly with \texttt{show} rebound. That is
\cref{prop:merge-asym}: the same two branches, merged in the two directions, give a failure and a
success.

\subsection{A rename raced by an edit, silently undone}\label{ex:merge-dup}

$\NA$ renames the formatter and stops. $\NB$ edits the formatter's body, and \cref{def:update}
propagates to \texttt{show}.
\[
  \NA = \{\texttt{price} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#f}}{0},\ \texttt{show} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#c}}{0}\}
  \qquad
  \NB = \{\texttt{fmt} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#f}'}{0},\ \texttt{show} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#c}'}{0}\}
\]
\[
\begin{array}{r@{\;}l@{\quad}l}
  \delta_{\NA} &= \{\texttt{fmt} \mapsto \ddel,\ \texttt{price} \mapsto \dadd\} & \\[3pt]
  \delta_{\NB}(\texttt{fmt}) &= \dupd & \\[3pt]
  \delta_{\NB}(\texttt{show}) &= \dupd
    & \just{$\namer$ separates \texttt{price} from \texttt{fmt}} \\[3pt]
  \texttt{fmt} &: (\ddel, \dupd) \mapsto \text{the update}
    & \just{\cref{def:mergecombine}, \cref{rem:updel}} \\[3pt]
  \NM &= \{\texttt{fmt} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#f}'}{0},\
            \texttt{price} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#f}}{0},\
            \texttt{show} \mapsto \UDer{\texttt{\#c}'}{0}\} &
\end{array}
\]
This merge succeeds, reports nothing, and is wrong in a way no diagnostic covers. The rename has been
undone --- \texttt{fmt} is back, because a delete loses to an update --- and its other half survived,
so \texttt{price} now names the \emph{superseded} version of the formatter. Where the ancestor had one
definition the result has two, one of them stale, and \texttt{show} calls the one the user meant to
rename away.

The three examples exhaust the ways a rename meets a concurrent edit, and none of them behaves as a
rename should: \cref{ex:merge-rename} reports a conflict that is not one, \cref{ex:merge-dangling}
fails with no conflict at all, and \cref{ex:merge-dup} succeeds and corrupts the namespace. The common
cause is \cref{def:mergediff}: it is a diff of two maps keyed by name, and a rename is the one edit
that a map keyed by name cannot represent.

\section{Comparison with arbor}\label{sec:contrast}

Everything above is about Unison. This section is the exception: it compares the design with
\emph{arbor}, a content-addressed computational substrate we are developing, for which a companion
formalization exists in the same style --- \arborcore{}, an untyped $\lambda$-calculus over a store, a
namespace and an evaluation cache, with an edit calculus; and \arborstlc{}, a simply-typed extension.
Readers who came for the Unison model can stop here.

arbor differs from Unison in ambition rather than in kind: it is a substrate intended to host several
languages, not a language. It shares the core commitments --- definitions identified by hash, names as
metadata, immutability, derived data keyed by hash --- and diverges on most of the mechanisms below.
Its formalization is machine-checked in Agda, which is why several rows can say ``proved''; that is a
statement about arbor's much smaller object language, not a claim that anything about Unison is
unproved-but-provable at the same cost.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.17\textwidth}p{0.38\textwidth}p{0.38\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Unison} & \textbf{arbor} \\
\midrule
Store key
& $\Hash \pfn \Comp$; a definition is $\UDer{h}{i}$ (\cref{def:ustore})
& $\Hash \pfn \Node$: one shallow node per hash, children by hash. A definition is a hash
  (\coreref{def:store}) \\
\addlinespace
Mutual recursion
& In the hasher. Presentation-independence is conditional, and the implementation aborts when the
  condition fails (\cref{thm:ordering}, \cref{rem:notpartial})
& Not yet reached. The leading candidate closes the cycle with a binder in the object language ---
  a fixpoint over a product, members as projections --- which leaves the store's type untouched \\
\addlinespace
Reference transparency
& Yes, at $\Pos = 0$ only (\cref{prop:transparent})
& Opaque in \coreref{def:node}; a decision to adopt transparency was taken in 2026-08 and mechanized
  on a branch, pending merge. Either way there is no positional asymmetry, there being no components \\
\addlinespace
Type
& Inside the term hash; a signature change is an identity change (\cref{def:typeinhash})
& A derived aspect \emph{about} a hash, observational rather than constitutive --- forced by wanting
  a store that can hold ill-typed and partially-written terms \\
\addlinespace
Primitives
& $\UBuiltin{b}$, named by text --- extrinsic (\cref{rem:builtin})
& Manually-versioned procedure tags --- also extrinsic, by a different mechanism \\
\addlinespace
Namespace
& A relation; conflict representable, unconflictedness demanded by the operations that need it
  (\cref{rem:conflict})
& A partial function; multiplicity pushed out into a family of namespaces, one per branch or agent \\
\addlinespace
Cache
& Keyed by a reference that need not denote anything stored (\cref{prop:watchdom}); no invalidation
  (\cref{cor:wnoinval})
& The same, including the argument (\coreref{cor:cache}). Neither system attaches an
  \emph{is-a-definition} status to stored data that the cache would have to respect \\
\addlinespace
Update mechanism
& Render $\to$ parse $\to$ typecheck $\to$ re-add (\cref{def:update})
& A structural rewrite over stored nodes; no surface syntax involved, so no round-trip obligation

\addlinespace

& Pin / follow / explicit over a scope axis --- a richer framing than the closest comparable system

\addlinespace

& Commit and report a residual set of names \\
\addlinespace

& \coreref{prop:print-elab}, proved --- and not depended upon \\
\bottomrule

\subsection*{Two observations we take from the comparison}

\textbf{On the round-trip property.} \Cref{prop:print-parse} identifies a correctness obligation that
a shipping system carries and maintains by testing. arbor happens to have the corresponding theorem
proved, for its own much smaller languages, and does not itself depend on it --- its migration is a
structural rewrite that never goes through surface syntax. So the honest form of the observation is
about the \emph{shape} of the guarantee and not about either system's rigor: one system depends on a
property it does not state, and the other states a property it does not depend on. What would be
genuinely useful, and is out of reach here, is a specification of the pretty-print environment under
which the property becomes provable at Unison's scale.

\textbf{On the failure case.} Of everything in the comparison, \cref{rem:residual} is the row where we
think Unison is ahead. arbor's migration commits and reports a residual --- a set of names that no
longer typecheck. Unison's answer is strictly more useful: the broken definitions leave the namespace
and come back as code, in a file, ready to edit. The namespace never holds a broken state, and the
report is the work itself rather than a description of it.

\section{Provenance, and how to check this}\label{sec:paths}

Everything here is modeled on Unison at commit \texttt{db60ce2} (2026-08-07). Re-check the pin with
\texttt{git -C <unison> rev-parse HEAD}; if it has moved, the citations below are still true \emph{at
that commit} --- they are dated, not necessarily wrong.

Separately from the citations, the arithmetic in this document has been executed. There is a
reimplementation of \crefrange{def:tokens}{def:update} over this object language, and each of the
statements in \crefrange{sec:ulang}{sec:uupdate} is a test in it that names the statement it checks.
\Cref{sec:umerge} is not yet part of that reimplementation; its evidence is of a different kind, and
is described two paragraphs below. It is not Unison and cannot
settle a question about Unison's intent; what it settles is whether our own definitions say what we
claim they say. Three things came back differently from how they were first written:
\cref{rem:transparency-limit} (confirmed, and at $k = 1$), \cref{thm:ordering}(b) (which had to
split, because renaming a tied member does not always move $\comphash$), and \cref{prop:print-parse}
(whose first implementation failed the property on a printer that dropped parentheses around an
application in argument position --- which is exactly the class of defect the golden corpus of
\cref{rem:printparse} exists to catch).

Two findings rest on direct observation rather than on the model. \Cref{rem:innercycle} is one: the
three hashes quoted there are what \texttt{ucm} 1.1.1 reports for the three definitions as written,
and the top-level abort is that tool refusing the same pair of bindings lifted out of the block. The
model reproduces the top-level behaviour and not yet the inner one.

\Cref{sec:umerge}'s evidence is direct in the same way. Each of \cref{ex:merge-rename},
\cref{ex:merge-dangling} and \cref{ex:merge-dup} was run against \texttt{ucm} 1.1.1 as a transcript
over three definitions, and the outcomes reported --- a conflict, a no-conflict failure naming a
deleted identifier, and a clean merge with the rename undone --- are what the tool produced, not what
the model predicts. \Cref{prop:merge-asym} was obtained the same way, by running one pair of branches
in both directions. This is weaker than the rest in one respect and stronger in another: it does not
show that our definitions are internally coherent, and it does show that the behavior is Unison's and
not an artifact of our reading.

Because Unison has no publication, the citable artifact for any claim about its behavior is the
source at a pinned commit, and we would encourage anyone building on this document to cite it that
way rather than citing a description.

Corrections are welcome, particularly from Unison's authors. Two points where the code
underdetermines the \emph{intent}, and where we are correspondingly least confident: whether the
position-$0$ boundary of \cref{prop:transparent} is deliberate, and whether the two orderings of
\cref{rem:twoorders} are meant to be distinct. Neither is a question about what the code does; both
are questions about whether it means to.

A third question, and the one we would most like answered: whether the interaction between renaming
and \cref{def:mergediff} is regarded as a defect. \Cref{cor:unchecked} shows the rename information
is computed and unused, and \cref{rem:libcanon} shows the one case where the resulting misclassification
was considered serious enough to patch around, so the phenomenon is clearly known. What we cannot tell
from the source is whether \cref{ex:merge-dup} --- a clean merge that undoes a rename and leaves a
stale alias --- is understood to follow from the same cause.

A fourth point is a claim rather than a question, and it contradicts a frequently-repeated
description of the system: \cref{rem:transparency-limit}, that a definition's \emph{component} hash
is not its \emph{term} hash, so the familiar illustration of reference transparency does not hold at
the level of whole definitions even though the term-level property does. The construction that
\emph{does} satisfy the stronger reading, \texttt{hashClosedTerm} \src{term:124-125}, is used for
anonymous and evaluated expressions and not for storing a named definition, which is what makes the
difference observable. We would still welcome confirmation, but we are no longer unsure of it: an
independent implementation of \cref{def:comphash} reproduces the gap, and reproduces it at $k = 1$
where it is easiest to check by hand.
