\begin{abstract}
Unison is a programming language whose codebase is content-addressed: a definition is identified by
the hash of its syntax, names are metadata attached to hashes rather than part of the code, and a
computed result is cached against a hash and never invalidated. These properties are widely cited.
As far as we can determine they have never been formally stated, and Unison has no peer-reviewed
publication, so the implementation is the only citable description of what it guarantees.

This document states them. We give a small object language --- the simply-typed $\lambda$-calculus
with $\TNat$ and $\TText$ --- and model, faithfully to the implementation at a pinned commit: hashing a strongly connected component of mutually recursive definitions; references as
$(\mathit{hash}, \mathit{position})$ pairs; reference transparency; the type inside the term hash;
the namespace as a relation in which conflicts are representable; the watch cache; and
\texttt{update}, which propagates an edit by rendering dependents to source, re-parsing, and
re-typechecking them.

Three results are perhaps not obvious from the source. \textbf{(i)} Presentation-independence of
component hashing is \emph{conditional}: when two members of a recursive cycle are structurally
equivalent modulo intra-cycle references, the component's hash depends on the members' \emph{names}.
The condition is decidable and Unison checks it, aborting rather than storing such a hash.
\textbf{(ii)} Reference transparency holds exactly at position $0$ of a component; other members are
referenced opaquely, so transparency and the component model are not independent features.
\textbf{(iii)} \texttt{update}'s correctness rests on printing-then-parsing preserving hashes, a
property the implementation does not state and maintains with a golden-file regression corpus.

A closing section compares the design with \emph{arbor}, a content-addressed substrate the authors
are developing, for which a companion formalization exists. That section is the only place arbor
appears; the rest of the document is about Unison alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:uscope}

\subsection{What Unison does differently}

In a conventional language a definition is identified by its name and located in a file. In Unison a
definition is identified by the hash of its own syntax, with names --- of its binders and of the
other definitions it mentions --- playing no part in that identity. The consequences that follow are
the system's design:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Names are metadata.} Renaming a definition does not change it, because its identity
        never mentioned the name. Nothing downstream is invalidated by a rename.
  \item \textbf{Definitions are immutable.} ``Editing'' a definition produces a different hash, so it
        is a different definition; existing callers continue to refer to the old one until something
        deliberately points them elsewhere.
  \item \textbf{Caches need no invalidation.} A computed value is stored against the hash of the
        expression that produced it. Since that hash denotes one fixed expression forever, the entry
        cannot go stale.
\end{itemize}

Mutually recursive definitions are the exception that shapes everything in \cref{sec:ustore}. A
member of a cycle cannot carry its neighbours' hashes, because computing any of them requires all of
them; so a cycle is hashed as a \emph{unit}, with its internal references standing as indices rather
than hashes. That single exception is why the store maps hashes to components rather than to
definitions, why a reference is a pair, and why \cref{def:comphash} needs a canonical ordering at
all.

The mechanism that makes this work also creates the rest of the problems the system has to solve, and
those are what this document is mostly about: names have to be reattached to hashes when code is
displayed, and propagating an edit to the code that depends on it is not a rename but a rebuild.

\subsection{Why formalize it}

Unison's claims about identity, immutability and caching are stated in its documentation and blog
posts as design intent. What is not available anywhere is a statement of them precise enough to
check, or to compare against another design --- what exactly is being identified with what, under
which conditions, and what has to hold for the conclusions to follow. Absent a publication, the
implementation is the specification, and the implementation is roughly 161{,}000 lines of Haskell.

We wrote this in order to compare Unison with a system we are building (\cref{sec:contrast}), and it
proved worth writing on its own terms. Several of the observations below --- the position-$0$
boundary of \cref{prop:transparent}, the two orderings of \cref{rem:twoorders}, the three
representations of \cref{rem:unhash} --- concern behaviour the code fixes precisely but which no
prose description we have found states. Writing down what a system does, in a form precise enough to
prove things about, turns out to be a different activity from reading it, and to surface different
things.

\subsection{Summary of results}\label{sec:summary}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \Cref{prop:transparent} --- reference transparency holds exactly at position $0$.
  \item \Cref{def:comphash}, \cref{rem:twoorders} --- component hashing computes two distinct
        orderings of the same component, one fixing de Bruijn indices and one fixing stored
        positions.
  \item \Cref{thm:ordering} --- presentation-independence of component hashing is conditional; when
        the condition fails the hash depends on the members' names.
  \item \Cref{cor:detect}, \cref{rem:notpartial} --- the condition is decidable, is checked, and the
        implementation aborts rather than store such a hash.
  \item \Cref{thm:ualpha} --- hashing identifies terms up to $\alpha$-equivalence and cycle
        permutation, and no further.
  \item \Cref{rem:unhash} --- a cyclic definition is stored in a different form from the one that
        was hashed, with self-references carrying no hash; an explicit inverse bridges them.
  \item \Cref{cor:wnoinval} --- no cache invalidation is required, as a consequence of immutability
        rather than as a heuristic.
  \item \Cref{prop:print-parse} --- \texttt{update} is correct only if printing inverts parsing.
  \item \Cref{prop:follow-only} --- \texttt{update} admits exactly one propagation strategy.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What this document is, and is not}

It is a model of an existing system, faithful to the implementation at commit \texttt{db60ce2}. It
proposes no changes. Where we describe something as a cost or a limitation we mean it descriptively:
every design pays somewhere, and pointing at where is not the same as saying it is wrong. The one
condition we identify that Unison treats as an error (\cref{cor:detect}) is already known and
tracked upstream as issue \#2787, and the implementation's handling of it --- detect and abort ---
is in our view the right response.

We are not modeling Unison's language. There are no abilities, no data declarations, no pattern
matching, no ANF runtime. \Cref{sec:unotmodeled} lists what is left out. Anything a Unison developer
finds surprising here is more likely to be our error than a discovery, and we would welcome
correction.

\subsection{The object language, and why it is typed}

The object language is the simply-typed $\lambda$-calculus with $\TNat$, $\TText$ and references
--- deliberately small, and typed for one reason. Unison's
\texttt{update} re-typechecks the code it rebuilds, and the interesting half of \texttt{update} is
what happens when that \emph{fails} --- Unison removes the broken definitions from the namespace and
hands them back to the user as editable source. Without a type system that branch is unreachable and
the most distinctive thing about the operation cannot be stated.

\subsection{What is modeled}

Stated as the operations a user can perform and what each one is observed to do, rather than as a
list of internal structures.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Ingest.} You present a group of definitions written in the object language. The
        system gives each one an identity derived from its content and stores it. Definitions that
        refer to one another cyclically receive their identities \emph{together}, which is the source
        of most of what follows.
  \item \textbf{Reference.} A stored definition may mention others. What it mentions is fixed when it
        is stored and cannot afterwards change, so no edit anywhere alters what an existing
        definition means.
  \item \textbf{Naming.} Names are attached to identities from outside, and are not part of what is
        stored. A name may denote more than one thing at once, and the system continues to function
        when it does.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation and caching.} A result is remembered against the identity of the
        expression that produced it, and never has to be discarded.
  \item \textbf{Update.} You present a new version of a definition. The system works out what
        depended on the old one, rebuilds those, and either accepts the result or tells you what
        broke --- by handing the broken definitions back to you as source.
\end{itemize}

Two things visible only through their consequences are modeled as well, because the rest does not
make sense without them: how the identity of a mutually recursive group is fixed
(\cref{def:comphash}), and the fact that a definition's recorded type is part of its identity
(\cref{def:typeinhash}).

\subsection{Not modeled}\label{sec:unotmodeled}

Stated so the boundary is explicit rather than implied.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{The namespace's representation.} Unison's \texttt{Branch} is a hashed Merkle tree of
        name segments whose \texttt{children} field points at each child's \emph{causal} hash, so
        every level of the tree has its own history \src{branchh:22-39}. Here the namespace
        is a bare relation. \Cref{sec:unaming} models the naming \emph{semantics}, which
        \texttt{update} needs; it does not model the object that carries them.
  \item \textbf{History, branching, and merge.} \texttt{Causal} as a content-addressed DAG, and the
        merge algorithm built over it.
  \item \textbf{Suffix resolution and the \texttt{lib}-depth priority rule.} Names here are atomic;
        in Unison a name is a sequence of segments and may be abbreviated to any unambiguous suffix.
  \item \textbf{Data declarations and \texttt{unique}-type minting.} With only $\TNat$, $\TText$ and
        arrows there are no declarations, so the $\UCon{\cdot}{\cdot}$ arm of $\Referent$ is present
        but vestigial, and the minting axis is absent entirely.
  \item \textbf{Synhash}, the provenance mechanism merge uses to distinguish a human edit from an
        automatic propagation.
  \item \textbf{Abilities, the ANF runtime, \texttt{LocalIds}, and the serialization layer.}
\end{itemize}

\section{The object language}\label{sec:ulang}

There are two grammars, and keeping them apart is the point of this section. A term as \emph{written}
mentions other definitions by name, and names are variables. A term as \emph{stored} mentions them by
reference, and a reference carries a hash. Name resolution is the map between the two, and it is
partial in a way that matters later.

In the implementation these are one datatype with one set of constructors; the distinction is which
constructors occur at which stage. Splitting them here is an idealization, and it is the idealization
that makes the rest of the document legible.

\subsection{Source terms}

\begin{definition}[Source terms]\label{def:uterm}
Fix a countable set of variables $x,y,z$.
\[
  e \;::=\; \uvar{x} \;\mid\; \ulam{x}{e} \;\mid\; \uapp{e}{e'}
        \;\mid\; \unat{n} \;\mid\; \utext{s} \;\mid\; \uann{e}{T}
\]
This is what a user writes. Note what is absent: there is no way to write a hash, and no term former
denotes a stored definition. A mention of another definition is a \emph{free variable}, exactly as a
mention of a local binding is a bound one, and the two are told apart only by an environment.
\end{definition}

\subsection{References}

\begin{definition}[Reference]\label{def:uref}
\src{ref:77-119} A reference denotes something already stored.
\[
  r \;::=\; \UBuiltin{b} \;\mid\; \UDer{h}{i}
  \qquad b \in \TText,\; h \in \Hash,\; i \in \Pos = \mathbb{N}
\]
$\UBuiltin{b}$ names a primitive, by text rather than by hash. $\UDer{h}{i}$ names the $i$-th member
of the group stored at $h$; \cref{def:ustore} explains why that is a pair.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Primitives are named extrinsically]\label{rem:builtin}
An identifier is \emph{intrinsic} when it can be recomputed from the thing it names and
\emph{extrinsic} when it is an assertion by some authority; a hash is the former and a builtin name
is the latter. So the system is intrinsically identified everywhere except at its primitives, and the
boundary is visible in the type of a reference rather than hidden in a convention. This is not
avoidable --- a hash chain must bottom out at things whose content the system does not model --- and
it is worth noting only because the sum type makes the seam unusually legible.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Resolved terms}

\begin{definition}[Resolved terms]\label{def:urterm}
\[
  t \;::=\; \uvar{x} \;\mid\; \ulam{x}{t} \;\mid\; \uapp{t}{u}
        \;\mid\; \unat{n} \;\mid\; \utext{s} \;\mid\; \uref{r} \;\mid\; \uann{t}{T}
\]
\Cref{def:uterm} extended with references. Binders still carry \emph{names}, and those names are
stored --- \cref{def:uhash} erases them only while hashing.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$\alpha$-equivalence]\label{def:ualpha}
$t \equiv_\alpha u$ is the least congruence identifying $\ulam{x}{t}$ with $\ulam{y}{t[x := y]}$ for
$y$ fresh in $t$. In the implementation it is the equality on terms, so $\alpha$-equivalent terms are
\emph{equal} before any hashing takes place.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Why storing binder names matters]\label{rem:names}
Storing de Bruijn indices and inventing names for display would produce the same hashes, so the
choice looks free. It is not. \Cref{sec:uupdate}'s \texttt{update} renders stored definitions back to
source that a human reads and a parser resolves, and because names survive storage, what is rendered
uses the \emph{author's} variable names. A system that discarded them could state its round trip only
up to a choice of names.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Name resolution]\label{def:uresolution}
\src{tparse:656; fparse:185-191} Given a namespace $\UNames$ (\cref{def:unames}) and a set $B$ of
names bound by the enclosing binding group, resolution $\mathcal{R}^{B}_{\UNames}$ maps a source term
to a resolved term, homomorphically except at variables:
\[
  \mathcal{R}^{B}_{\UNames}(\uvar{x}) \;=\;
    \begin{cases}
      \uvar{x} & x \in B \text{, or } x \text{ locally bound}\\
      \uref{r} & \UNames(x) = \{\URefT{r}\}\\
      \uvar{x} & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Resolution is deliberately partial]\label{rem:staging}
Three of these cases leave a variable in place, and only one produces a reference. That is not an
omission.

A name bound by the group being ingested \emph{cannot} be resolved --- its referent does not exist
yet, and for a recursive group it cannot exist until the group is hashed (\cref{sec:comphash}).
Resolution therefore stops at the boundary of what is already stored, and the remaining variables are
discharged by ingest.

The last case is more interesting: a name that resolves to nothing, or to more than one thing, is
left free \emph{on purpose}. The implementation's comments are explicit --- ``not found: leave free
for telling user about expected type'' and ``ambiguous: leave free for TDNR'' --- so an unresolved
name survives into typechecking, where the expected type may disambiguate it or produce a better
error than resolution could. Failure to resolve is not an error in itself.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Types and typing}

\begin{definition}[Types and typing]\label{def:utyping}
\[
  T,U \;::=\; \TNat \;\mid\; \TText \;\mid\; \uarr{T}{U}
\]
Typing is over \emph{resolved} terms. A context $\Gamma$ maps variables to types; a signature
$\Delta$ maps references to types --- given for builtins, and read off the store for derived
references (\cref{def:typeinhash}). Syntax-directed, with no inference:
\begin{mathpar}
  \inferrule*[right=U-Var]{\Gamma(x) = T}{\utypes{\Gamma}{\uvar{x}}{T}}
  \and
  \inferrule*[right=U-Nat]{\ }{\utypes{\Gamma}{\unat{n}}{\TNat}}
  \and
  \inferrule*[right=U-Text]{\ }{\utypes{\Gamma}{\utext{s}}{\TText}}
  \\
  \inferrule*[right=U-Abs]{\utypes{\Gamma, x{:}T}{t}{U}}{\utypes{\Gamma}{\ulam{x}{t}}{\uarr{T}{U}}}
  \and
  \inferrule*[right=U-App]{\utypes{\Gamma}{t}{\uarr{T}{U}} \\ \utypes{\Gamma}{u}{T}}
                          {\utypes{\Gamma}{\uapp{t}{u}}{U}}
  \\
  \inferrule*[right=U-Ref]{\Delta(r) = T}{\utypes{\Gamma}{\uref{r}}{T}}
  \and
  \inferrule*[right=U-Ann]{\utypes{\Gamma}{t}{T}}{\utypes{\Gamma}{\uann{t}{T}}{T}}
\end{mathpar}
A term with a free variable is not typeable, which is how \cref{rem:staging}'s unresolved names
become errors --- at typechecking, with a type to report, rather than at resolution.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Binding groups}

\begin{definition}[Binding group]\label{def:bindgroup}
A \emph{binding group} is a finite map $\bg$ from names to resolved terms, whose members' free
variables all lie in $\dom(\bg)$. Ingest takes a binding group, not a single term.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Why ingest is group-at-a-time]\label{rem:mutrec}
There is no recursive term former. Mutual recursion is several bindings that mention one another, so
the unit that can be given identities is the group, not the definition. Everything distinctive about
\cref{sec:ustore} follows from this: with single-term ingest every component would be a singleton and
\cref{def:comphash} would be invisible.

The closedness condition is what \cref{rem:staging} left over --- the variables resolution could not
discharge are exactly the group's own names, and ingest discharges them by hashing.
\end{remark}

\section{The store and the hash}\label{sec:ustore}

\subsection{The store and its components}

\begin{definition}[Component and store]\label{def:ustore}
 A \emph{component} is a
non-empty finite sequence of terms, $\Comp = \Term^{+}$ --- a strongly connected component of the
dependency graph, hashed as a unit. The store is
\[
  \UStore : \Hash \pfn \Comp .
\]
A definition is addressed by a \emph{pair}: $\UDer{h}{i}$ denotes the $i$-th member of the component
at $h$. A non-recursive definition is a singleton component with $i = 0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[The store is not hash-to-definition]\label{rem:storetype}
It is natural to summarize content addressing as ``a definition is identified by its hash'', and for
non-recursive code that is what happens. But the map is from a hash to a \emph{component}, and a
definition is a pair. Mutual recursion is the reason: a member of a cycle cannot be hashed before its
neighbours, since it refers to them, and they refer back. Hashing the cycle as a unit breaks the
knot, at the price of making every reference positional and every property below a property of
components rather than of definitions.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Hashing}

\begin{definition}[Token stream]\label{def:tokens}
 Nothing is hashed as raw bytes. The input is a tagged,
length-prefixed token stream: $\mathsf{Tag}$ is one byte; $\mathsf{Text}$ and $\mathsf{Bytes}$ are a
64-bit big-endian length followed by the payload; $\mathsf{Nat}$ is 64-bit big-endian;
$\mathsf{Hashed}$ is a fixed-width hash with no length prefix. Every stream begins with a
\emph{version} token, and each syntactic layer begins with a tag domain-separating it from the others
. Write $\mathsf{acc}(\overline{\tau})$ for the hash of a token list.
The function is SHA3-512, untruncated.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Versioning the hash function]\label{rem:version}
The leading version token exists so that changing the hash function changes \emph{every} hash --- if
it did not, simple values would collide across generations in a single global hash table. Underneath,
the schema carries a many-hashes-to-one-object indirection so that hashes computed under an old
scheme keep resolving . This is a comparatively rare thing for a
content-addressed system to plan for, and worth recording: the migration story for ``we changed the
hash'' is versioning plus indirection, not recomputation.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Hashing a term]\label{def:uhash}
 Hashing carries an environment
$\HEnv \in (\wp(\Name) + \Name)^{*}$, innermost first, where $\mathsf{R}(x)$ is an ordinary binder and
$\mathsf{L}(V)$ is a \emph{cycle frame} naming a whole set at once. Write $\uhash{\HEnv}{t}$.
\begin{align*}
  \uhash{\HEnv}{\uvar{x}}    &= \mathsf{acc}[\mathsf{Nat}\; j]
     && \text{$j$ least with $x \in \HEnv_j$; undefined if none}\\
  \uhash{\HEnv}{\ulam{x}{t}} &= \mathsf{acc}[\ldots,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\;\uhash{\mathsf{R}(x)\cdot\HEnv}{t}]\\
  \uhash{\HEnv}{\uapp{t}{u}} &= \mathsf{acc}[\ldots,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\;\uhash{\HEnv}{t},\ \mathsf{Hashed}\;\uhash{\HEnv}{u}]
\end{align*}
and correspondingly at the other formers. Three details are load-bearing. Binder \emph{names are
discarded} on the way in, so $\alpha$-equivalent terms hash equally. \emph{Annotations are excluded by
construction} --- ``we ignore annotations in the \texttt{Term}, as these should never affect the
meaning of the term'' . And a variable bound by a cycle frame
$\mathsf{L}(V)$ hashes to the frame's index, \emph{the same index for every member of $V$}; that is
what \cref{def:comphash} exploits. A free variable is an error.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The type is inside the term hash]\label{def:typeinhash}
\src{term:92-109} Before hashing, each member of a binding group is wrapped in its type,
$t \mapsto \uann{t}{T}$, replacing an existing top-level annotation if there is one. The hash of a
definition is therefore a function of its type as well as its body, and a change of signature is a
change of identity.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[The exception that proves the rule]\label{rem:typeinhash}
There is a \texttt{hashTermComponentsWithoutTypes}  used where no type
is available. Its existence is the clearest statement that the usual path deliberately puts the type
in: hashing without the type is possible, and is treated as the special case.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[\textsc{U-Ref} is a lookup, not a re-derivation]\label{rem:uref-delta}
Typing a reference costs nothing: $\Delta$ is consulted and no derivation about the target is
required. That is affordable precisely because the type is inside the hash
(\cref{def:typeinhash}) --- a stored definition's recorded type cannot disagree with the definition,
since changing the type would change the hash and therefore produce a different definition. The store
admits no ill-typed entry, so there is nothing for \textsc{U-Ref} to check.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Reference transparency, and its boundary}

\begin{proposition}[Transparency holds exactly at position $0$]\label{prop:transparent}
\src{term:135-146} In the term layer,
\[
  \uhash{\HEnv}{\uref{\UDer{h}{0}}} = h,
  \qquad
  \uhash{\HEnv}{\uref{\UDer{h}{i}}} = \mathsf{acc}[\mathsf{Tag}\;1,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\;h,\ \mathsf{Nat}\;i]
  \quad (i \neq 0).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[What transparency does, and where its boundary falls]\label{rem:transparent}
For $i = 0$ --- every non-recursive definition, and one distinguished member of each cycle
(\cref{sec:comphash} assigns the positions) --- a reference contributes \emph{literally} the
referent's hash. A reference to position $0$ is therefore interchangeable, inside any enclosing hash,
with any term that hashes to $h$.

For $i \neq 0$ it is opaque, tagged with its position. So transparency and the component model are
not independent features; the boundary between them falls at $\Pos = 0$. Whether that is deliberate
--- inlining a singleton component is free, so there is a plausible reason --- or an artifact of the
encoding, we do not know; the source comment justifies only the $0$ case.

\Cref{ex:transparency} works through what this does and does not give, and the answer is narrower
than the usual summary of it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Component hashing}\label{sec:comphash}

This is the deepest mechanism in the document and the one whose properties are least apparent from
the code.

\begin{definition}[Component hashing]\label{def:comphash}
\src{abt:73-100, 175-217} Let $C = \langle (y_1,u_1),\dots,(y_k,u_k)\rangle$ be a
component, \emph{listed in name order} --- the implementation always obtains it as
$\mathsf{Map.toList}$. Let $V = \{y_1,\dots,y_k\}$.

\emph{Step 1 --- permutation hashes.} In the environment $\HEnv_p = \mathsf{L}(V)$, every intra-cycle
reference resolves to the same index, so member bodies become comparable without any of them yet
having an identity:
\[
  \phash{y_j} \;=\; \uhash{\HEnv_p}{u_j}.
\]

\emph{Step 2 --- the binding order.} Sort the members by $\phash{\cdot}$, \textbf{stably}, giving a
permutation $\pi$; set $\HEnv = \mathsf{R}(y_{\pi(1)})\cdots\mathsf{R}(y_{\pi(k)})$. Each member now
has a distinct de Bruijn index, and the member hashes are $h_j = \uhash{\HEnv}{u_{\pi(j)}}$.

\emph{Step 3 --- the storage order.} With
$\overline{\tau} = \mathsf{Tag}\;1,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\;h_1,\dots,\mathsf{Hashed}\;h_k$ shared by every
member, put
\[
  \nhash{y} \;=\; \mathsf{acc}\bigl(\overline{\tau} \;+\!\!+\; [\mathsf{Hashed}\;\uhash{\HEnv}{\uvar{y}}]\bigr),
\]
so a member is disambiguated \emph{only} by its own index in $\HEnv$. Sort the members by
$\nhash{\cdot}$; that order is the component's storage order, the position $i$ in $\UDer{h}{i}$
counts in it, and
\[
  \comphash(C) \;=\; \mathsf{acc}[\mathsf{Hashed}\;\nhash{y_{\sigma(1)}},\dots,\mathsf{Hashed}\;\nhash{y_{\sigma(k)}}]
\]
for $\sigma$ the sorting permutation.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Two orders, not one]\label{rem:twoorders}
$\pi$ and $\sigma$ are different permutations of the same component. $\pi$ fixes the de Bruijn
indices used \emph{while hashing} member bodies; $\sigma$ fixes the $\Pos$ indices that appear \emph{in
stored references}. They coincide only by accident, since $\sigma$ sorts by a hash of the $\pi$-index.
Nothing observable depends on the difference --- re-hashing re-derives $\HEnv$ from scratch --- but a
reimplementation that conflated them would produce different hashes for the same code, and no prose
description of the algorithm we are aware of mentions that there are two.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Ingest]\label{def:uingest}
 $\hashcomps(\bg)$ decomposes $\bg$ into strongly connected components in
topological order and folds \cref{def:comphash} over them, substituting $\UDer{h}{i}$ for the names of
already-hashed components before hashing the next. The store is extended by each component at its
hash.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[A cyclic definition is not stored in the form that was hashed]\label{rem:unhash}
Three representations of an intra-component reference have to be kept apart, and the difference is
invisible unless one looks for it.

\emph{While hashing}, a reference from one cycle member to another is a \textbf{variable}, resolved
by \cref{def:uhash} to an index into the cycle frame. It has to be: the component's hash is what is
being computed.

\emph{In memory}, once \cref{def:uingest} has finished, those variables have been substituted by
$\UDer{h}{i}$ carrying the component's own freshly computed hash.

\emph{Serialized}, a self-reference is stored with \textbf{no hash at all}. The stored type is
$\mathsf{Reference}'\;\mathsf{Text}\;(\mathsf{Maybe}\;\mathsf{Hash})$ --- ``a possibly-self
(R = \emph{recursive}) reference'' --- with $\mathsf{Nothing}$ marking a reference into the enclosing
component \src{ref:51-52, 89-90}.

Consequently the bytes on disk are \emph{not} the bytes that were hashed, and re-deriving a stored
component's hash requires undoing the substitution first. That operation exists and is documented as
such: \texttt{unhashComponent} ``replaces all \texttt{Nothing} self-referential hashes with a
variable reference to the relevant piece of the component'' \src{decl:80-93}, and the hash-validation
path calls it before re-hashing \src{termhash:26-42}.

None of this is observable through the API, and it is a sensible encoding --- writing a component's
own hash into its members' serialized bytes would mean the bytes could not be produced until the hash
was known, which is the circularity the whole construction exists to break. It is worth stating
because ``the hash of a definition is the hash of what is stored'' is a natural thing to assume, and
for cyclic definitions it is false in three distinct ways.
\end{remark}

\subsection{What component hashing guarantees}

\begin{theorem}[Conditional presentation-independence]\label{thm:ordering}
Let $C$ and $C'$ be the same component listed in two different orders.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\alph*)]
  \item If $\phash{\cdot}$ is \emph{injective} on $V$, then $\comphash(C) = \comphash(C')$, the member
        hashes agree, and each member receives the same $\Pos$.
  \item If $\phash{\cdot}$ is not injective, $\comphash$ depends on the listed order. Since the list is
        always obtained in name order, the component's hash --- and every reference into it ---
        depends on the members' \textbf{names}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$\HEnv_p$ mentions no member individually, so $\phash{y}$ is a function of $u_y$ alone and is
unaffected by the listing. (a) If $\phash{\cdot}$ is injective, sorting by it is a total order on $V$
determined by the members' bodies, so $\pi$, hence $\HEnv$, hence every $h_j$, hence every
$\nhash{\cdot}$, hence $\sigma$ and $\comphash$, are all listing-independent. (b) If
$\phash{y} = \phash{y'}$ for $y \neq y'$, the sort in Step 2 is stable and therefore returns them in
listed order; exchanging them in the listing exchanges their de Bruijn indices, which changes
$\nhash{\cdot}$ and in general $\comphash$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The side condition is decidable, and is checked]\label{cor:detect}
\src{abt:186-192, 208-217} Injectivity of $\phash{\cdot}$ on $V$ is decided by grouping the
members by permutation hash. \texttt{doHashCycle} does exactly this and emits
\texttt{IncompleteElementOrderingError}, listing the structurally equivalent sets.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Total function, conditional guarantee, aborting caller]\label{rem:notpartial}
It is tempting to summarize \cref{thm:ordering} as ``canonical ordering is a partial function'' and
``Unison refuses''. Both are slightly off, and the accurate version is more interesting.

$\comphash$ is a \emph{total} function: on the ambiguous input it returns a hash. What fails is not
definedness but \emph{presentation-independence} --- the property content addressing exists to
provide. The fallback is alphabetical order of user-chosen names, which is precisely the kind of
dependency the design is meant to eliminate.

The refusal is at the \emph{caller}. \texttt{doHashCycle} returns the condition as a warning, and its
own comment says ``hashing always succeeds even if it generates warnings'';
\texttt{crashOnHashingWarning} turns it into an exception, and the main term-component path takes that
route \src{ufile:284}, with a top-level handler rendering it as bug \texttt{E253299} plus a
workaround --- add a distinguishing binding such as \texttt{\_ = "this is the foo definition"}
\src{abt:55-62; main:116}. Internal \texttt{let rec} bindings deliberately ignore
the warning, because there the ordering is not observable \src{abt:167-172}.

So the accurate statement is: \emph{a decidable side condition guards presentation-independence, and
the implementation aborts rather than store a hash that violates it.} That is a stronger engineering
position than a partial function --- the system never silently produces a name-dependent identity ---
and a sharper obligation for a formalism, because the side condition has to appear in the statement of
every downstream result about component identity. The condition is tracked upstream as
unisonweb/unison\#2787.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Hashing collapses $\alpha$-equivalence and cycle permutation]\label{thm:ualpha}
Let $\phash{\cdot}$ be injective on $V$. Then $\comphash$ identifies two components exactly when they
are related by $\alpha$-equivalence of members (\cref{def:ualpha}) together with a permutation of the
cycle. It does not identify observationally equivalent members.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$(\Leftarrow)$ Binder names are discarded by \cref{def:uhash}, and permutation-independence is
\cref{thm:ordering}(a). $(\Rightarrow)$ By injectivity of $\mathsf{acc}$ on token streams and the
domain-separating tags of \cref{def:tokens}, downward through the structure.
\end{proof}

\section{Naming}\label{sec:unaming}

Names are not part of a definition, and nothing above consulted one. This section says what they are
attached to and what a namespace is, which is as much as \cref{sec:uupdate} needs; the object that
carries a namespace, and its history, are out of scope (\cref{sec:unotmodeled}).

\begin{definition}[Referent]\label{def:ureferent}
 $\Referent ::= \URefT{r} \mid \UCon{r}{k}$. Names denote
\emph{referents}, not references: a name may denote a data constructor rather than a definition. With
no data declarations in scope (\cref{sec:unotmodeled}) the second arm is vestigial here, and is
retained only so that \cref{def:unames} has the right shape.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Namespace]\label{def:unames}
\src{branchh:22-39; sql:271} A namespace is a \emph{relation}
\[
  \UNames \;\subseteq\; \Name \times \Referent ,
\]
written $\UNames(x)$ for $\{\,\varrho \mid (x,\varrho) \in \UNames\,\}$. One name may denote several
referents and one referent may have several names; both are ordinary states rather than errors. The
database agrees --- the name-lookup key includes the referent columns, so several rows per name are
legal.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Unconflictedness]\label{def:unconf}
 $\unconf{\UNames}{x}$ holds iff
$|\UNames(x)| = 1$, and $\unconf{\UNames}{}$ holds iff it holds at every name in $\dom(\UNames)$.
When it does, $\UNames$ determines a partial function and we write
$\uresolve{\UNames}{x}{\varrho}$. It is computed as a derived view, recomputed rather than
maintained.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Conflict is representable; operations demand its absence]\label{rem:conflict}
Making conflict a legal state rather than an error at bind time is what lets a merge complete without
resolving everything first: the namespace can hold both candidates and the user can sort it out
afterwards. It is worth noting where the cost lands. \texttt{update} cannot proceed against a
conflicted namespace and \emph{asserts} unconflictedness before doing anything
\src{update:99-102}. And Unison's own forward-looking
\texttt{BranchV3} format removes conflicted names outright --- ``a V3 branch is a trimmed-down V2
branch: Names can't be conflicted'' \src{branchv3:16-30}. So the generality is
real, and the direction of travel appears to be away from it.
\end{remark}

\section{The evaluation cache}\label{sec:ucache}

\begin{definition}[Watch cache]\label{def:watch}
The cache is a finite partial function $\UCache : \Hash \pfn \Hash$, from the hash of an expression to
the hash of its value. Both are store hashes.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[The key need not be a definition]\label{rem:watchkey}
$\dom(\UCache)$ is the hash of an \emph{expression}, which need not be named and need not be anything
the user declared. There is no notion of ``is a definition'' attached to stored data that the cache
would have to respect; a hash is a hash. This is what lets a watch expression in a scratch file be
cached on the same footing as a top-level binding.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Cache soundness is stable under growth]\label{thm:wstable}
If $\UCache(h) = h_v$ is sound in $\UStore$ and $\UStore \subseteq \UStore'$, it is sound in
$\UStore'$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Evaluating $h$ reads only entries reachable from $h$; all of them are in $\dom(\UStore)$ and, by
immutability, identical in $\UStore'$. The argument uses neither well-formedness nor anything about
the namespace.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[No invalidation is needed]\label{cor:wnoinval}
An entry, once written, is valid in every future store. Consequently no invalidation logic is
required --- and none exists. The only eviction paths in the implementation are a debug-only
wholesale delete and a garbage collection of orphaned hashes.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[What ``never invalidated'' actually means]\label{rem:cachemeaning}
This is the claim Unison is best known for, and it is worth stating precisely because the informal
version invites a weaker reading. ``Caches are never invalidated'' is not an empirical observation
that definitions rarely change, and not a policy that could be traded away for freshness. It is a
consequence of two facts: the store is append-only, and the cache is keyed by a hash of exactly the
expression that was evaluated. Together they make staleness unrepresentable. Nothing in the argument
mentions names, types, or how often anyone edits anything.
\end{remark}

\section{Update}\label{sec:uupdate}

\subsection{Dependents}

\begin{definition}[Dependents]\label{def:dependents}
$\deps(h,i)$ is the set of references occurring in the $i$-th member of the component at $h$;
$\dependents$ is its converse, maintained as an index populated when a definition is saved. Note that
this index can exist because a reference remains a node in the stored representation even though it
hashes transparently at position $0$ (\cref{prop:transparent}): transparency is a property of the
hash, not of the representation.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Update scope]\label{def:uscope}
\src{ops:1125-1126; update:139-179} Given a set $Q$ of references being updated and a
namespace $\UNames$,
\[
  \tdeps(Q) \;=\; \bigl(\dependents^{+}(Q) \;\cap\; \mathcal{S}\bigr) \setminus (F \cup L)
\]
where $\dependents^{+}$ is the transitive closure excluding self-references, $\mathcal{S}$ is the set
of references having an \emph{unconflicted} name in $\UNames$, $F$ is what the incoming file itself
shadows, and $L$ is everything under $\lib$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Naming is what puts a definition in scope]\label{rem:scopenaming}
An unnamed definition is not a dependent to be updated, even if it references the target. Naming is
the only thing that makes a stored object a participant in an edit --- which is a second place, after
\cref{rem:watchkey}, where the system declines to attach a ``definition'' status to stored data and
lets the namespace carry it instead.

Note also what is \emph{absent}: there is no type-based gating. Unison formerly classified each edit
as $\mathsf{Same} \mid \mathsf{Subtype} \mid \mathsf{Different}$ and propagated only the first two
automatically; that machinery was removed in 2025. The current design computes the closure and lets
the typechecker decide, reporting failure rather than predicting it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Update, as rendering}

\begin{definition}[Pretty-print environment]\label{def:ppe}
 A $\ppe$ assigns each reference a name to
render it by, under two different suffixification strategies: definitions \emph{in the file} are
suffixified by name, definitions \emph{in the codebase} by hash. The asymmetry is forced --- aliases
sharing a suffix may be referred to by that suffix in the codebase but not in a file, where the parser
reports an ambiguity. The source gives the example: with \texttt{one.foo} and \texttt{two.foo} both in
the file, \texttt{hey = foo + foo} fails to parse.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Update]\label{def:update}
 Given a typechecked incoming file $F$ and a
configuration $\UConfig{\UStore}{\UNames}{\UCache}$ with $\unconf{\UNames}{}$:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Let $Q$ be the references of the names $F$ redefines, and $D = \tdeps(Q)$.
  \item Render: $S = \render(\ppe, D)$, source text for every dependent.
  \item Parse and typecheck $F \cup S$ \emph{as one file}.
  \item If it typechecks, ingest (\cref{def:uingest}) and rebind every affected name.
  \item If it does not, \textbf{remove} the offending definitions' names from $\UNames$ and deliver
        $S$ to the user as editable source on a dedicated update branch.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[The failure case is the interesting one]\label{rem:residual}
Step 5 is the part of \texttt{update} least anticipated by informal descriptions of it. When
propagation fails, the namespace is not left holding broken definitions and the operation does not
roll back: the broken definitions are \emph{evicted} from the namespace, and their source becomes the
user's working state. The namespace stays coherent by construction, and the unfinished work is the
scratch file. Note the dependency on \cref{rem:names} --- what is handed back has to be readable, so
binder names must have survived storage.

The same closure computation feeds \texttt{merge}, which puts conflicted definitions and their
dependents into a scratch file together for the same reason: a conflict cannot be resolved in
isolation, because whatever the user picks, everything downstream must be re-typechecked against it.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Update propagates by exactly one strategy]\label{prop:follow-only}
The transition of \cref{def:update} admits no parameter controlling propagation: $\tdeps$ is fixed.
Every transitive dependent with an unconflicted name outside $\lib$ is rebuilt, and there is no way to
request that some callers be left pointing at the old definition. The nearest thing to that is to
decline to run \texttt{update} and bind a new name with \texttt{add} instead, which is abstention
rather than an operation. A per-definition choice does reappear at \emph{failure} time, since whatever
fails to typecheck comes back as source for the user to edit.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{The obligation update rests on}

\begin{proposition}[Update is correct only if printing inverts parsing]\label{prop:print-parse}
Let $d$ be a dependent not redefined by $F$, and let $d'$ be the definition obtained from step 4 for
$d$. The intended semantics of \cref{def:update} is that $d'$ differs from $d$ \emph{only} by having
its references to $Q$ replaced by their new targets. This holds iff, for every $d \in D$,
\[
  \uparse(\ppe,\ \render(\ppe, d)) \;=\; d
  \qquad\text{modulo exactly that replacement.}
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Step 3 reconstructs $d'$ from text alone. Any discrepancy introduced by the round trip --- a binder
renamed so as to change capture, an identifier resolving to a different alias, a different
associativity --- is preserved into $d'$ and changes its hash for a reason unrelated to the update.
Conversely, if the round trip is exact except at the references being retargeted, $d'$ is by
construction the intended substitution.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The property is understood precisely, and lives in a test]\label{rem:printparse}
The implementation's own comment for this step is: ``We are updating old references to new references
by rendering old references as names that are then parsed back to resolve to new references (the
world's weirdest implementation of AST substitution)''
\src{update:424-427}. The correctness property that comment
presupposes does not appear as a statement anywhere in the source. It is instead \emph{tested}:
\texttt{unison-src/transcripts-round-trip/} renders a regression corpus, re-adds it to a fresh
namespace, and asserts that the namespace diff is empty --- ``this diff should be empty if the two
namespaces are equivalent. If it's nonempty, the diff will show us the hashes that differ.'' The
corpus file is named \texttt{reparses-with-same-hash.u}, and its sibling \texttt{reparses.u} holds the
weaker cases that need only parse.

The naming is the point. The property is understood exactly, and the distinction between ``reparses''
and ``reparses with the same hash'' is drawn in the file names --- it is simply maintained as a golden
corpus rather than stated as an invariant. Given the size of Unison's surface language that is a
defensible engineering choice. \Cref{prop:print-parse} makes explicit what the corpus is protecting,
and the difficulty demonstrably concentrates in one place: the pretty-print environment of
\cref{def:ppe}.
\end{remark}

\section{Worked examples}\label{sec:examples}

Symbolic hashes are written \texttt{\#a}, \texttt{\#b}, and $\ulcorner t \urcorner$ abbreviates
$\uhash{\HEnv}{t}$ where the environment is clear. \texttt{Nat.+} is a builtin, so it resolves to
$\UBuiltin{\texttt{Nat.+}}$ and never acquires a hash of its own.

\subsection{Ingesting one definition}\label{ex:ingest}

The user writes
\[
  \texttt{inc} \;=\; \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Nat.+}}}{\uvar{n}}}{\unat{1}}}
\]
and the pipeline of \cref{sec:ulang,sec:ustore} runs as follows.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=\arabic*.]
  \item \textbf{Parse.} Both \texttt{Nat.+} and \texttt{n} are variables (\cref{def:uterm}); \texttt{n}
        is bound by the $\lambda$, \texttt{Nat.+} is free.
  \item \textbf{Resolve} (\cref{def:uresolution}). \texttt{Nat.+} is not in the binding group and the
        namespace maps it to a builtin, so it becomes $\uref{\UBuiltin{\texttt{Nat.+}}}$. The result
        is a resolved term with no free variables.
  \item \textbf{Typecheck.} $\Delta$ gives \texttt{Nat.+} the type $\uarr{\TNat}{\uarr{\TNat}{\TNat}}$,
        so \texttt{inc} has type $\uarr{\TNat}{\TNat}$.
  \item \textbf{Wrap} (\cref{def:typeinhash}). The term hashed is not the body but
        $\uann{(\ulam{n}{\ldots})}{\uarr{\TNat}{\TNat}}$.
  \item \textbf{Component} (\cref{def:comphash}). \texttt{inc} does not mention itself, so its
        strongly connected component is the singleton $\{\texttt{inc}\}$ and
        \cref{def:comphash} runs at $k = 1$: the permutation sort is trivial, $\HEnv$ is one binder,
        and the member hash is $h_1 = \ulcorner \uann{(\ulam{n}{\ldots})}{\uarr{\TNat}{\TNat}}
        \urcorner$.
  \item \textbf{Positions.} $\nhash{\texttt{inc}} = \mathsf{acc}[\mathsf{Tag}\,1,\
        \mathsf{Hashed}\,h_1,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\,\ulcorner \uvar{\texttt{inc}}\urcorner]$ and the
        component hash is $\texttt{\#a} = \mathsf{acc}[\mathsf{Hashed}\,\nhash{\texttt{inc}}]$.
  \item \textbf{Store and name.} The component is stored at \texttt{\#a}; the definition is
        $\UDer{\texttt{\#a}}{0}$; and only now is the name \texttt{inc} attached to it, in the
        namespace and not in the definition.
\end{enumerate}

Step 6 is worth pausing on: \textbf{the component hash is not the member's hash.} There are two
layers between them --- the shared token prefix and the outer accumulation --- even when the
component has one member. \Cref{ex:transparency} is where that matters.

\subsection{A reference, and what transparency gives}\label{ex:transparency}

Now two definitions in one group, the second a bare alias of the first:
\[
  \texttt{x} \;=\; \uapp{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Nat.+}}}{\unat{1}}}{\unat{1}}
  \qquad\qquad
  \texttt{y} \;=\; \uvar{\texttt{x}}
\]
\texttt{y} depends on \texttt{x}, so the strongly connected components are $\{\texttt{x}\}$ then
$\{\texttt{y}\}$, in that order. Ingesting \texttt{x} as in \cref{ex:ingest} gives a component hash
\texttt{\#x}, with the definition at $\UDer{\texttt{\#x}}{0}$. Ingest then substitutes that reference
for the variable \texttt{x} before hashing \texttt{y}, so the term hashed for \texttt{y} is
\[
  Y \;=\; \uann{\uref{\UDer{\texttt{\#x}}{0}}}{\TNat},
  \qquad\text{against}\qquad
  X \;=\; \uann{(\uapp{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Nat.+}}}{\unat{1}}}{\unat{1}})}{\TNat} .
\]
By \cref{prop:transparent} the reference contributes \texttt{\#x}, so
$\ulcorner Y \urcorner = \mathsf{acc}[\ldots,\ \mathsf{Hashed}\,\texttt{\#x},\
\mathsf{Hashed}\,\ulcorner\TNat\urcorner]$, while $\ulcorner X \urcorner$ has
$\ulcorner \uapp{\uapp{\ldots}{\unat 1}}{\unat 1} \urcorner$ in that position.

\begin{remark}[The usual summary of transparency is too strong]\label{rem:transparency-limit}
The source comment illustrates transparency with exactly this pair, saying that \texttt{x = 1 + 1}
and \texttt{y = x} ``hash the same''. On the reading above they do not, and the reason is
\cref{ex:ingest}'s step 6.

$\ulcorner X \urcorner = \ulcorner Y \urcorner$ would require
$\ulcorner \texttt{Nat.+ 1 1} \urcorner = \texttt{\#x}$ --- the referent's \emph{term} hash to equal
its \emph{component} hash. But \texttt{\#x} is built \emph{from} $\ulcorner X \urcorner$ by two
accumulations, so the equation asks the hash function for a fixed point of a particular shape.
Transparency passes along the component hash; inlining would contribute the term hash;
\cref{def:comphash} puts two layers between them. So the two definitions receive different hashes,
and inlining a reference into a larger term does not preserve that term's hash either.

What \emph{is} true is what \cref{prop:transparent} says: a position-$0$ reference is
indistinguishable from any term hashing to \texttt{\#x}. The comment's own caveat is precisely this
condition --- ``so long as \texttt{Reference.Derived} ctors are created using the same hashing
function as is used here''. There is a construction that satisfies it, \texttt{hashClosedTerm}, which
builds $\UDer{\ulcorner t \urcorner}{0}$ directly from a term's hash \src{term:124-125}; references
made that way \emph{are} interchangeable with their referents. The component construction is not that
way.

We flag this as the place we are least confident of our reading, since it bears on a
frequently-repeated description of the system. If the definition-level claim does hold, we have
misread the relationship between \texttt{overallHash} and the member hashes.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A recursive group}\label{ex:cycle}

\[
  \texttt{countdown} \;=\; \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{countup}}}{\uvar{n}}}
  \qquad
  \texttt{countup} \;=\; \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{countdown}}}{(\uapp{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Nat.+}}}{\uvar{n}}}{\unat 1})}}
\]
Each mentions the other, so they form one component of size two and neither can be resolved to a
reference before the other exists (\cref{rem:staging}). \Cref{def:comphash} runs in three steps.

\emph{Permutation hashes.} In $\HEnv_p = \mathsf{L}(\{\texttt{countdown},\texttt{countup}\})$ both
names hash to the \emph{same} index, so the bodies are compared with their mutual references made
indistinguishable:
\[
  \phash{\texttt{countdown}} = \ulcorner \ulam{n}{\uapp{\langle 1\rangle}{\langle 0 \rangle}} \urcorner
  \qquad
  \phash{\texttt{countup}} = \ulcorner \ulam{n}{\uapp{\langle 1\rangle}{(\uapp{\uapp{\cdot}{\langle 0\rangle}}{\unat 1})}} \urcorner
\]
writing $\langle j \rangle$ for the index $j$. The bodies differ --- one applies \texttt{Nat.+} ---
so the two hashes differ and the sort is decided by structure alone, not by the names
\texttt{countdown} and \texttt{countup}.

\emph{Binding order.} Say the sort puts \texttt{countdown} first. Then
$\HEnv = \mathsf{R}(\texttt{countdown})\,\mathsf{R}(\texttt{countup})$, each member now has its own
index, and the member hashes $h_1, h_2$ are computed in that environment.

\emph{Storage order.} $\nhash{\cdot}$ is computed for each name and the members are sorted
\emph{again} by it. This second sort is by a hash of the first sort's index, so it need not agree
with it (\cref{rem:twoorders}): \texttt{countup} may well end up at position $0$ and
\texttt{countdown} at position $1$.

The component is stored at some \texttt{\#c}, and the two definitions are $\UDer{\texttt{\#c}}{0}$
and $\UDer{\texttt{\#c}}{1}$. \textbf{Whichever landed at position $1$ is now referenced opaquely}
(\cref{prop:transparent}), while its partner at position $0$ is referenced transparently --- a
difference between two definitions that the user wrote symmetrically and cannot observe.

\subsection{When the canonical order is ambiguous}\label{ex:ambiguous}

Change the example so the two members differ only by which of the other they call:
\[
  \texttt{ping} \;=\; \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{pong}}}{\uvar{n}}}
  \qquad
  \texttt{pong} \;=\; \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{ping}}}{\uvar{n}}}
\]
In $\HEnv_p$ both mutual references collapse to the same index, so the two bodies become the
\emph{same} term $\ulam{n}{\uapp{\langle 1 \rangle}{\langle 0 \rangle}}$ and
\[
  \phash{\texttt{ping}} \;=\; \phash{\texttt{pong}} .
\]
$\phash{\cdot}$ is not injective, so \cref{thm:ordering}(b) applies. The sort is stable, the list
arrived in name order, and \texttt{ping} therefore precedes \texttt{pong} --- \textbf{for no reason
but the alphabet}. Renaming \texttt{ping} to \texttt{zing} would swap the binding order and change
the component's hash.

The implementation detects the collision (\cref{cor:detect}) and aborts rather than store a
name-dependent identity, reporting bug \texttt{E253299} and suggesting the user distinguish the two
definitions by hand --- for instance by adding \texttt{\_ = "this is the ping definition"}. That
suggestion is exactly a request to make $\phash{\cdot}$ injective again.

\subsection{An update that succeeds}\label{ex:update-ok}

Stored, from two earlier ingests:
\[
  \texttt{scale} \;=\; \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Nat.+}}}{\uvar n}}{\uvar n}}
  \qquad
  \texttt{double} \;=\; \ulam{n}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{scale}}}{\uvar n}}
\]
with \texttt{scale} at $\UDer{\texttt{\#s}}{0}$, \texttt{double} at $\UDer{\texttt{\#d}}{0}$, and
\texttt{double}'s stored body containing $\uref{\UDer{\texttt{\#s}}{0}}$.

The user edits \texttt{scale} to $\ulam{n}{\uapp{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Nat.*}}}{\uvar n}}{\unat 2}}$,
of the same type, and runs \texttt{update} (\cref{def:update}).

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=\arabic*.]
  \item $Q = \{\UDer{\texttt{\#s}}{0}\}$, and $\tdeps(Q) = \{\UDer{\texttt{\#d}}{0}\}$ ---
        \texttt{double} depends on \texttt{scale} and has an unconflicted name.
  \item \texttt{double} is \emph{rendered back to source} under a pretty-print environment
        (\cref{def:ppe}). Its stored body holds a hash; what is printed is the name \texttt{scale}.
  \item The file --- the new \texttt{scale} plus the printed \texttt{double} --- is parsed and
        typechecked as a unit. This time \texttt{scale} resolves to the \emph{new} definition, which
        is the entire mechanism by which the reference is retargeted.
  \item It typechecks. Both are ingested, yielding \texttt{\#s'} and \texttt{\#d'}, and both names are
        rebound.
\end{enumerate}

Note what changed and what did not. \texttt{double}'s \emph{source text} is character-for-character
what it was; its \emph{hash} changed, because the definition it names changed. And the old
\texttt{\#d} is still in the store, still referring to the old \texttt{\#s}, still meaning exactly
what it meant --- nothing was mutated, and any cached result for \texttt{\#d} remains valid
(\cref{cor:wnoinval}).

\subsection{An update that fails}\label{ex:update-fail}

Same starting state, but the user changes \texttt{scale} to something of a different type:
$\ulam{s}{\uapp{\uvar{\texttt{Text.size}}}{\uvar s}}$, of type $\uarr{\TText}{\TNat}$.

Steps 1--3 run as before. At step 3 the file does not typecheck: \texttt{double} applies
\texttt{scale} to its argument $n$, and $n$ is used at $\TNat$. Step 5 of \cref{def:update} then
applies:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \texttt{scale} is ingested and its name rebound to the new definition.
  \item \texttt{double}'s name is \textbf{removed from the namespace}.
  \item The rendered source for \texttt{double} is handed back to the user, on an update branch, as
        the thing to edit.
\end{itemize}

The namespace is left coherent --- it contains no name denoting a definition that fails to typecheck
--- and the unfinished work is a file. The old \texttt{\#d} has not been deleted or altered; it has
merely stopped being reachable by name, which is the only kind of ``deletion'' the store supports.

\Cref{rem:residual} discusses why this is the design's most distinctive feature, and
\cref{prop:print-parse} is the obligation step 2 rests on: the rendering of \texttt{double} must be
something the parser turns back into \texttt{double}, differing only in what \texttt{scale} resolves
to.

\section{Comparison with arbor}\label{sec:contrast}

Everything above is about Unison. This section is the exception: it compares the design with
\emph{arbor}, a content-addressed computational substrate we are developing, for which a companion
formalization exists in the same style --- \arborcore{}, an untyped $\lambda$-calculus over a store, a
namespace and an evaluation cache, with an edit calculus; and \arborstlc{}, a simply-typed extension.
Readers who came for the Unison model can stop here.

arbor differs from Unison in ambition rather than in kind: it is a substrate intended to host several
languages, not a language. It shares the core commitments --- definitions identified by hash, names as
metadata, immutability, derived data keyed by hash --- and diverges on most of the mechanisms below.
Its formalization is machine-checked in Agda, which is why several rows can say ``proved''; that is a
statement about arbor's much smaller object language, not a claim that anything about Unison is
unproved-but-provable at the same cost.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.17\textwidth}p{0.38\textwidth}p{0.38\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Unison} & \textbf{arbor} \\
\midrule
Store key
& $\Hash \pfn \Comp$; a definition is $\UDer{h}{i}$ (\cref{def:ustore})
& $\Hash \pfn \Node$: one shallow node per hash, children by hash. A definition is a hash
  (\coreref{def:store}) \\
\addlinespace
Mutual recursion
& In the hasher. Presentation-independence is conditional, and the implementation aborts when the
  condition fails (\cref{thm:ordering}, \cref{rem:notpartial})
& Not yet reached. The leading candidate closes the cycle with a binder in the object language ---
  a fixpoint over a product, members as projections --- which leaves the store's type untouched \\
\addlinespace
Reference transparency
& Yes, at $\Pos = 0$ only (\cref{prop:transparent})
& Opaque in \coreref{def:node}; a decision to adopt transparency was taken in 2026-08 and mechanized
  on a branch, pending merge. Either way there is no positional asymmetry, there being no components \\
\addlinespace
Type
& Inside the term hash; a signature change is an identity change (\cref{def:typeinhash})
& A derived aspect \emph{about} a hash, observational rather than constitutive --- forced by wanting
  a store that can hold ill-typed and partially-written terms \\
\addlinespace
Primitives
& $\UBuiltin{b}$, named by text --- extrinsic (\cref{rem:builtin})
& Manually-versioned procedure tags --- also extrinsic, by a different mechanism \\
\addlinespace
Namespace
& A relation; conflict representable, unconflictedness demanded by the operations that need it
  (\cref{rem:conflict})
& A partial function; multiplicity pushed out into a family of namespaces, one per branch or agent \\
\addlinespace
Cache
& Keyed by any expression hash; no invalidation (\cref{cor:wnoinval})
& The same, including the argument (\coreref{cor:cache}) \\
\addlinespace
Update mechanism
& Render $\to$ parse $\to$ typecheck $\to$ re-add (\cref{def:update})
& A structural rewrite over stored nodes; no surface syntax involved, so no round-trip obligation
  arises \\
\addlinespace
Propagation
& One strategy, scope fixed (\cref{prop:follow-only})
& Pin / follow / explicit over a scope axis --- a richer framing than the closest comparable system
  ships, which is worth knowing before treating it as descriptive of prior art \\
\addlinespace
Update failure
& Evict the broken names, hand back source (\cref{rem:residual})
& Commit and report a residual set of names \\
\addlinespace
Round-trip lemma
& Load-bearing, unstated, maintained by a golden corpus (\cref{rem:printparse})
& \coreref{prop:print-elab}, proved --- and not depended upon \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection*{Two observations we take from the comparison}

\textbf{On the round-trip property.} \Cref{prop:print-parse} identifies a correctness obligation that
a shipping system carries and maintains by testing. arbor happens to have the corresponding theorem
proved, for its own much smaller languages, and does not itself depend on it --- its migration is a
structural rewrite that never goes through surface syntax. So the honest form of the observation is
about the \emph{shape} of the guarantee and not about either system's rigor: one system depends on a
property it does not state, and the other states a property it does not depend on. What would be
genuinely useful, and is out of reach here, is a specification of the pretty-print environment under
which the property becomes provable at Unison's scale.

\textbf{On the failure case.} Of everything in the comparison, \cref{rem:residual} is the row where we
think Unison is ahead. arbor's migration commits and reports a residual --- a set of names that no
longer typecheck. Unison's answer is strictly more useful: the broken definitions leave the namespace
and come back as code, in a file, ready to edit. The namespace never holds a broken state, and the
report is the work itself rather than a description of it.
